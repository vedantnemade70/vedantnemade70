# LAW OF CRIMINAL PROCEDURE (BNSS, 2023): Module-wise Explanation

> The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) came into force on **1 July 2024** and replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC). It has **531 sections**. Old CrPC numbers are given in brackets because most books and judgments still use them.

---

## MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO BNSS, 2023

### 1. Why we need criminal procedure
Criminal law has two parts:
- **Substantive law** (the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, BNS) says *what* is a crime and *what* the punishment is.
- **Procedural law** (the BNSS) says *how* the law is enforced: how a crime is reported, investigated, how the accused is arrested, tried, sentenced, and how appeals are made.

Without procedure, substantive law is only words on paper. Procedural law must also keep a balance:
- **crime control**: the State must catch and punish offenders efficiently;
- **due process**: the accused must be treated fairly, and the innocent must be protected.

The BNSS tries to balance both, and also gives a new place to the **victim**.

### 2. Adversarial and inquisitorial systems
There are two broad models of criminal justice.

**Adversarial (accusatorial) system** (England, USA, India):
- The prosecution and the defence are opponents.
- The judge is a **neutral umpire**.
- The accused is **presumed innocent**, and the prosecution must prove guilt **beyond reasonable doubt**.
- Evidence is collected by the parties and tested by **cross-examination**.

**Inquisitorial system** (France, Germany):
- The judge actively **investigates** and questions witnesses to find the truth.
- Written dossiers matter more than oral contest.

India follows the adversarial model but has **inquisitorial features**:
- the court can summon any witness (s.348, CrPC 311);
- the judge can ask any question (BSA s.168);
- the court examines the accused (s.351, CrPC 313).

In *Zahira Habibullah Sheikh v. State of Gujarat* (2004) (the Best Bakery case), the Supreme Court said the judge is not a mere spectator but must actively help find the truth. The Malimath Committee (2003) also recommended adding more inquisitorial elements.

### 3. International norms and constitutional basis
**International instruments:**
- **UDHR (1948)**: Arts. 9–11, which cover no arbitrary arrest, a fair hearing, and the presumption of innocence.
- **ICCPR (1966)**: Arts. 9, 14 and 15, which cover prompt production before a judge, legal aid, no self-incrimination, no double jeopardy, and no ex post facto law.

**The Indian Constitution builds these protections in:**
- **Art. 14**: equality; procedure must not be arbitrary.
- **Art. 20**:
  - 20(1): no retrospective criminal law;
  - 20(2): no double jeopardy;
  - 20(3): no self-incrimination (*Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani*, 1978; *Selvi v. State of Karnataka*, 2010, which held narco-analysis cannot be done without consent).
- **Art. 21**: after *Maneka Gandhi v. UOI* (1978), any procedure depriving liberty must be "fair, just and reasonable". From this the courts derived:
  - the right to a speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979);
  - free legal aid (*M.H. Hoskot*, 1978);
  - protection from custodial torture (*D.K. Basu*, 1997).
- **Art. 22**: the right to know the grounds of arrest, to consult a lawyer, and to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
- **Art. 39A**: free legal aid.

### 4. Salient features of the BNSS
The BNSS keeps most of the CrPC structure but introduces:
- **Zero FIR and e-FIR** (s.173).
- **Preliminary enquiry** for offences punishable with 3–7 years.
- **Mandatory forensic** examination for offences punishable with 7 years or more (s.176).
- **Videography** of search and seizure (s.105).
- **Trial in absentia** of proclaimed offenders (s.356).
- **Community service** as a punishment for petty offences (s.23).
- **Fixed timelines**:
  - copies of documents in 14 days;
  - charge within 60 days of first hearing;
  - judgment within 30 days of arguments, extendable to 45;
  - the victim told of the progress of investigation within 90 days.
- **Relief for undertrials**: a first-time offender is released after serving one-third of the maximum sentence (s.479).
- **Deemed sanction** to prosecute a public servant if not decided in 120 days (s.218).
- **Directorate of Prosecution** (s.20).
- **Witness Protection Scheme** (s.398).
- **Handcuffing** for certain serious offenders (s.43(3)).
- **Attachment of proceeds of crime** (s.107).
- **Electronic** summons, trials and evidence.
- **Metropolitan Magistrates and Assistant Sessions Judges abolished**.

### 5. Organisation of functionaries
- **Police**: prevent crime, register FIRs, investigate, arrest, search, file the chargesheet.
- **Prosecution** (ss.18–20):
  - Public Prosecutors (PP) need 7 years of practice; Special PPs need 10 years.
  - Assistant Public Prosecutors (APP) appear in magistrates' courts.
  - The new **Directorate of Prosecution** supervises prosecution work.
  - The PP is a "minister of justice", not a persecutor. They must place all evidence fairly (*Shiv Kumar v. Hukam Chand*, 1999).
  - A victim may engage their own lawyer to assist the PP.
- **Defence**: every accused has the right to a lawyer of choice (s.340) and to legal aid at State expense (s.341).
- **Prison authorities**: keep custody and work towards reform. Prisoners do not lose their fundamental rights (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Admin.*, 1978).

### 6. Important definitions (s.2)
- **Bail**: release from custody on conditions, on a bond. This is defined for the first time.
- **Bailable offence**: shown as bailable in the First Schedule. All others are non-bailable.
- **Cognizable offence**: police can arrest without warrant. **Non-cognizable offence**: they cannot.
- **Complaint**: an allegation to a **Magistrate**, oral or written, asking them to take action. It does not include a police report.
- **Investigation**: evidence collection by police.
- **Inquiry**: anything other than a trial done by a Magistrate or court.
- **Trial**: ends in conviction or acquittal.
- **Warrant case**: an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment **above 2 years**. **Summons case**: all other offences.
- **Victim**: includes the guardian or legal heir.

### 7. Hierarchy and powers of courts (ss. 6–29)
Section 6 lists the classes of criminal courts:
1. Court of Session
2. Judicial Magistrate First Class (JMFC)
3. Judicial Magistrate Second Class
4. Executive Magistrates

The High Court and Supreme Court sit above them. The Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) heads the magistracy in a district and is subordinate to the Sessions Judge (s.13).

**Sentencing powers:**

| Court | Maximum sentence |
|---|---|
| High Court | Any sentence authorised by law |
| Sessions Judge | Any sentence; **death needs High Court confirmation** |
| CJM | Up to 7 years |
| JMFC | 3 years, fine up to ₹50,000, or community service |
| JM-II | 1 year, fine up to ₹10,000, or community service |

When there are several sentences in one trial, the aggregate cannot exceed 20 years (s.25).

### 8. Aid to police and magistrates (ss. 30–34)
- Superior police officers have the same powers as the officer in charge of a police station (s.30).
- Every member of the public is **bound to assist** a magistrate or police officer in arresting an offender, preventing a breach of peace, or protecting public property (s.31).
- The public must report certain serious offences (s.33).
- Village officers must report suspicious deaths and the presence of proclaimed offenders (s.34).

## MODULE 2: JURISDICTION OF CRIMINAL COURTS IN INQUIRIES AND TRIALS

### 1. General principle: local jurisdiction (s.197)
The basic rule is that **every offence is inquired into and tried by the court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed** (s.197, CrPC 177). The reasons are:
- witnesses and evidence are available locally;
- it is convenient for the parties;
- the local community sees justice done.

### 2. Exceptions and special rules (ss. 198–209)
Crimes do not always happen neatly in one place, so the BNSS gives flexibility:
- **Uncertain or multiple places (s.198)**: where it is uncertain where the offence was committed, or it was committed partly in one area and partly in another, or it is a **continuing offence**, any of those courts can try it.
- **Act or consequence (s.199)**: an offence is triable where the act was done **or** where the consequence followed. For example, if a person is injured in one place and dies in another, either court can try the case.
- **Related offences (s.200)**: abetment or receiving stolen property can be tried where either act happened.
- **Special places (s.201)**:
  - dacoity or murder with dacoity: where committed or where the accused is found;
  - kidnapping: where the person was taken, kept or concealed;
  - theft: where the stolen property is possessed;
  - criminal breach of trust: where the property was received or should have been accounted for.
- **Letters and electronic communication (s.202)**: triable where sent or received. **Bigamy** is triable where the offender or the first spouse resides.
- **Offences during a journey (s.203)**: triable in any area the person or goods passed through.
- **Doubt (s.206)**: the High Court decides.
- **Offences outside India (s.208)**: committed by an Indian citizen anywhere, or on an Indian ship or aircraft. They are triable where the accused is found in India, but need **prior sanction of the Central Government**.

In *Y. Abraham Ajith v. Inspector of Police* (2004), the Supreme Court said the civil concept of "cause of action" does not apply to criminal jurisdiction. In *Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod* (2014), it fixed jurisdiction in cheque bounce cases.

### 3. Initiation of proceedings: cognizance (ss. 210–214)
**"Taking cognizance"** means the magistrate **applies their judicial mind** to the facts in order to proceed further (*S.K. Sinha v. Videocon*, 2008). Cognizance is of the **offence**, not the offender.

Under s.210, a magistrate takes cognizance on:
(a) a **complaint**;
(b) a **police report**; or
(c) **information from any other person** or the magistrate's **own knowledge**.

In case (c), the accused can ask for a transfer to another magistrate (s.211).

A **Court of Session cannot take cognizance directly**. The case must be **committed** to it by a magistrate (s.213).

### 4. Exceptions to the general power of cognizance (ss. 215–222)
For some offences, the law protects certain persons or interests by requiring a **complaint or sanction**:
- **s.215 (CrPC 195)**: offences against public servants' authority, offences against public justice, and forgery of documents produced in court. These need a **written complaint by the public servant or court** concerned (*Iqbal Singh Marwah v. Meenakshi Marwah*, 2005).
- **s.217**: offences against the State and certain conspiracies need **Government sanction**.
- **s.218 (CrPC 197)**: prosecution of judges and public servants for official acts needs **prior sanction**. Under the BNSS, sanction is **deemed granted if not decided in 120 days**.
- **s.219**: offences relating to marriage need a complaint by the **aggrieved person**.
- **s.220**: cruelty by husband or relatives needs a police report or a complaint by the victim or her relatives.
- **s.222**: **defamation** needs a complaint by the person defamed. Where a high constitutional functionary is defamed, the Public Prosecutor files the complaint in a Sessions Court.

### 5. Complaint to magistrate (ss. 223–226)
When a complaint is filed:
1. The magistrate **examines the complainant and witnesses on oath** (s.223). Under a new BNSS proviso, **the accused must be heard before cognizance is taken**.
2. If in doubt, the magistrate may **postpone process** and inquire personally or order a police investigation (s.225). This is **mandatory if the accused lives outside the magistrate's jurisdiction** (*Vijay Dhanuka*, 2014).
3. If there is no sufficient ground, the complaint is **dismissed** with brief reasons (s.226).

### 6. Commencement of proceedings (ss. 227–233)
If there is sufficient ground:
- the magistrate issues **summons** (in a summons case) or a **warrant** (in a warrant case) (s.227);
- may exempt the accused from personal appearance (s.228);
- in petty cases, allows a plea of guilty by post (s.229).

The accused must receive **free copies of the police report and documents within 14 days** (s.230). This is part of a fair trial.

Cases exclusively triable by a Sessions Court are **committed** to it, ideally within 90 days (s.232).

Where a complaint case and a police investigation on the same matter are running at the same time, the complaint case is stayed and both are tried together (s.233).

## MODULE 3: INFORMATION TO POLICE, INVESTIGATION AND ARREST

### 1. FIR (s.173)
The **First Information Report** is the information about a **cognizable offence** first given to the police. It sets the criminal law in motion.
- It may be given **orally or electronically**. An e-FIR must be signed within 3 days.
- It can be lodged at **any police station (Zero FIR)**, which transfers it to the right station.
- The informant gets a **free copy**.
- Offences against women are recorded by a **woman officer**.
- For offences punishable with 3–7 years, a **preliminary enquiry of up to 14 days** is allowed.
- If police refuse to register an FIR, the informant can go to the **Superintendent of Police** and then to the **magistrate (s.175(3))**, with an affidavit.

In ***Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of U.P.* (2014)**, the Constitution Bench held that registration of an FIR is **mandatory** when the information discloses a cognizable offence.

**Evidentiary value:**
- An FIR is **not substantive evidence**. It is used to **corroborate** or **contradict** the informant.
- If the informant dies, it can be a **dying declaration**.
- A confession in an FIR given by the accused is **inadmissible** (*Aghnoo Nagesia*, 1966).
- **Delay** in filing is not fatal if explained.

For **non-cognizable offences**, the police only record the information and send the informant to the magistrate. They cannot investigate without the magistrate's order (s.174).

### 2. Investigation and interrogation (ss. 175–193)
- The officer in charge may investigate a cognizable case **without the magistrate's order** (s.175).
- Police can **summon witnesses** (s.179). Women, children under 15, persons above 60 and disabled persons are questioned **at their homes**.
- Statements of witnesses (s.180) are **not signed** and **cannot be used as evidence** except to **contradict** the witness (s.181, CrPC 162).
- Police must not use threats or inducements (s.182).
- A rape victim is medically examined within 24 hours (s.184).

### 3. Arrest
**Arrest without warrant (s.35, CrPC 41).** Police may arrest without a warrant when:
- a cognizable offence is committed **in their presence**;
- for offences punishable with **up to 7 years**, there is credible information **and arrest is necessary**: to prevent further offences, for proper investigation, to stop tampering with evidence or witnesses, or to secure attendance. **Reasons must be recorded**;
- for offences punishable with more than 7 years, there is credible information;
- the person is a proclaimed offender or similar.

Where arrest is not necessary, a **notice of appearance** is issued (old s.41A). Persons who are **infirm or above 60** cannot be arrested for offences below 3 years without a DSP's permission.

***Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014)** and ***Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P.* (1994)** prohibit routine, automatic arrests.

**How arrest is made (s.43):**
- by touching or confining the person;
- a woman is arrested only by a woman officer, and **not after sunset or before sunrise** except with a magistrate's permission;
- **handcuffs** only for specified serious or habitual offenders;
- **no more restraint than necessary** (s.46).

A **private person** can arrest someone who commits a non-bailable and cognizable offence in their presence, but must hand them to the police within 6 hours (s.40).

**Arrest with warrant (ss. 72–83):** a warrant is written, signed and sealed, and remains in force until executed or cancelled. It may be **bailable** (s.73). The arrested person must be produced before the court within **24 hours**.

**Proclamation and attachment (ss. 84–93):** if the accused **absconds**, the court issues a **proclamation** requiring appearance within at least 30 days. The person may be declared a **proclaimed offender** and their **property attached** (s.85). Property abroad may also be attached (s.86).

Police must report all warrantless arrests to the District Magistrate (s.59). An arrested person is released only on bond, bail or a magistrate's order (s.60). Police may **pursue and re-arrest** an escaped person anywhere in India (s.61).

### 4. Rights of arrested persons
These come from ss.47–58, 340–341 and Art. 22, and from *D.K. Basu*:
1. To know the **grounds of arrest**. Following *Pankaj Bansal* (2023) and *Prabir Purkayastha* (2024), these must be given **in writing**.
2. To be told of the **right to bail** in bailable offences.
3. To have a **relative or friend informed**.
4. To **meet a lawyer** during interrogation (s.38).
5. To be **produced before a magistrate within 24 hours** (ss.57–58).
6. To a **medical examination** (s.53).
7. To a **decent search**, with a woman searched by a woman.
8. To **free legal aid**.
9. To **compensation** for illegal detention (*Rudul Sah*, 1983; *Nilabati Behera*, 1993).

### 5. Preventive powers of police (ss. 168–172)
Police must try to **prevent** cognizable offences. They may **arrest to prevent** one if there is no other way, but cannot detain for more than 24 hours (s.170). They may prevent injury to public property (s.171). Under the new s.172, they may **detain or remove persons who resist lawful directions**.

### 6. Search and seizure (ss. 49, 96–110, 185–186)
- Courts issue **search warrants** (ss.96–99).
- A search must be made in the presence of **two independent local witnesses**, and a list of seized items prepared.
- The BNSS requires **audio-video recording** of search and seizure (s.105).
- During investigation, an officer may search **without a warrant** if delay would defeat the purpose. They must **record reasons** and send copies to the magistrate within 48 hours (s.185).
- s.107 allows **attachment of proceeds of crime**.
- Evidence from an illegal search is **not automatically inadmissible** but is weighed with caution (*Radha Kishan v. State of U.P.*, 1963).

### 7. Confessions and statements before a magistrate (s.183)
A Judicial Magistrate may record a confession or statement during investigation. The safeguards are:
- the magistrate must **warn** the accused that they are not bound to confess and that the confession may be used against them;
- the magistrate must be satisfied it is **voluntary**;
- the accused is given time for reflection;
- if the accused refuses, they must not be sent back to police custody.

The statement may be recorded by audio-video. A woman victim's statement is recorded by a **woman magistrate** where possible.

### 8. Remand (s.187)
If investigation cannot be completed in 24 hours, the accused is produced before a magistrate, who may authorise detention.
- **Police custody** is up to 15 days, which may now be taken **in parts during the first 40 or 60 days**. This changes *CBI v. Anupam Kulkarni* (1992), which allowed police custody only in the first 15 days.
- **Total detention** cannot exceed **90 days** (offences punishable with death, life or 10 years or more) or **60 days** (other offences).
- If the chargesheet is not filed in time, the accused gets **default bail** as of right (*Sanjay Dutt*, 1994; *M. Ravindran*, 2021).

### 9. Police diary and police report (ss. 192–193)
**Case diary (s.192):** the investigating officer keeps a **day-by-day diary**. The court may use it to aid the trial, but it is **not evidence**. The accused cannot demand it, except when the police officer uses it to refresh memory (*Shamshul Kanwar*, 1995).

**Police report (s.193):** after investigation, a **chargesheet or closure report** is sent to the magistrate.
- Investigation in rape cases must be finished in 2 months.
- The victim must be told of progress within 90 days.
- **Further investigation** is allowed, but if done during trial, it needs the court's permission and must be completed in 90 days.
- On a closure report, the informant must be **heard** (*Bhagwant Singh*, 1985).

### 10. Inquest (ss. 194–196)
In cases of suicide, homicide, accidental or suspicious death, police hold an **inquest** in the presence of two respectable persons and report the **apparent cause of death** within 24 hours. A **post-mortem is compulsory** for deaths of women within 7 years of marriage.

A **magistrate's inquiry** is compulsory for deaths or disappearances in **police custody** (s.196).

An inquest only finds the apparent cause of death, not who is guilty (*Pedda Narayana*, 1975).

## MODULE 4: BAIL, SECURITY AND MAINTENANCE

### 1. Bail (ss. 478–496)
Bail protects the **presumption of innocence** and personal liberty under Art. 21. Justice Krishna Iyer put it as "**bail is the rule, jail the exception**" (*State of Rajasthan v. Balchand*, 1977).

**(a) Bailable offences (s.478).** Bail is a **right**, and police or the court *must* release the accused. If the accused cannot furnish bail within a week, they are presumed **indigent** and released on a personal bond.

**(b) Maximum detention of undertrials (s.479).** An undertrial who has spent **half** the maximum sentence in custody must be released. A **first-time offender** is released after **one-third**. This does not apply to offences punishable with death or life.

**(c) Non-bailable offences (s.480).** Bail is **discretionary**. A magistrate should not grant bail where there are reasonable grounds to believe the person committed an offence punishable with death or life, **except** for a woman, a child, or a sick or infirm person. Conditions may be imposed.

**(d) Anticipatory bail (s.482).** A person who **fears arrest** for a non-bailable offence may apply to the **High Court or Sessions Court** for a direction that they be released on bail if arrested.
- *Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia* (1980) gave courts wide discretion.
- *Sushila Aggarwal* (2020) held anticipatory bail is ordinarily **not time-limited**.
- It is not available for rape or gang rape of minors.

**(e) Special powers of the High Court and Sessions Court (s.483).** These courts can grant bail in any case and **cancel bail** if the accused misuses liberty, tampers with evidence or threatens witnesses.

**Factors courts consider:**
- nature and gravity of the offence;
- severity of punishment;
- strength of evidence;
- chance of absconding;
- chance of tampering with evidence;
- criminal record.

***Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI* (2022)** laid down bail guidelines.

### 2. Security for keeping peace and good behaviour (ss. 125–143)
These are **preventive** provisions. They stop a crime before it happens; they do not punish.
- **After conviction** for breach of peace offences, the court may take a bond for **up to 3 years** (s.125).
- An **Executive Magistrate** may require a person likely to commit a breach of peace to give a bond for **up to 1 year** (s.126).
- Good behaviour bonds may be taken from:
  - persons spreading certain harmful matters (s.127);
  - **suspected persons** hiding to commit offences (s.128);
  - **habitual offenders**, for up to **3 years** (s.129).

**Procedure:** a written show-cause order setting out the information; an inquiry; then a bond or discharge. Failing to give security leads to imprisonment.

These powers must be used strictly, because they restrict liberty without a crime having been committed (*Madhu Limaye*, 1970).

### 3. Public order: unlawful assemblies (ss. 148–151)
An Executive Magistrate or police officer may order an **unlawful assembly to disperse**. If it does not, **civil force** may be used. If necessary, the **armed forces** may be used, causing as little injury as possible. Officers acting in good faith are **protected from prosecution** without Government sanction.

### 4. Public nuisance (ss. 152–163)
Under s.152, a magistrate may pass a **conditional order** to remove a public nuisance, such as:
- an obstruction on a public road;
- an unhealthy trade;
- a dangerous building;
- an unfenced well;
- a dangerous animal.

The person must comply or show cause. The court in *Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardichan* (1980) held that a municipality cannot plead lack of funds.

**Section 163 (old s.144)** allows the magistrate to pass **urgent orders** to prevent danger, riot or disturbance of public peace. The order lasts **2 months** and can be extended by the State to **6 months**.
- *Madhu Limaye* (1970) upheld the power as constitutional.
- *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020) held it must be **proportionate**, reasoned and not used to suppress legitimate expression.

### 5. Disputes about immovable property (ss. 164–167)
When a land or water dispute is likely to cause a **breach of peace**, the Executive Magistrate decides **who is in actual possession**, not who owns it. A person wrongfully dispossessed within **2 months** is treated as being in possession.

The property can be **attached** in an emergency (s.165). Disputes about **right of use** (such as a right of way) are covered in s.166. **Title is left to the civil court.**

### 6. Maintenance of wives, children and parents (ss. 144–147)
This is a **quick, secular remedy** to prevent **destitution and vagrancy**. It applies to every religion.

**Who can claim (s.144):** a person who has **sufficient means** must maintain:
- their **wife**, including a **divorced wife who has not remarried**, if she is unable to maintain herself;
- **minor children**, legitimate or illegitimate;
- **major children** who are unable to maintain themselves because of physical or mental abnormality;
- their **father and mother**.

**Amount and procedure:**
- The magistrate fixes a monthly allowance. There is **no upper limit**.
- **Interim maintenance** can be granted, and the application should be decided in 60 days.
- A wife loses the right if she lives in **adultery**, refuses to live with her husband **without sufficient reason**, or they live apart by **mutual consent**. A husband marrying again or keeping a mistress is sufficient reason for her to live separately.
- **Alteration** is allowed if circumstances change (s.146).
- **Enforcement** (s.147) is by a warrant to recover the money as a fine, and imprisonment of up to **1 month for each month unpaid**. The claim must be made **within 1 year** of the amount falling due.

**Key cases:**
- ***Shah Bano* (1985)**: s.125 CrPC applies to Muslim women.
- ***Danial Latifi* (2001)**: upheld the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. The husband must make a **reasonable and fair provision for her whole future**, paid within the iddat period.
- ***Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana* (2024)**: a divorced Muslim woman can **also** claim under s.125 CrPC (now s.144 BNSS).
- ***Rajnesh v. Neha* (2020)**: guidelines. Affidavits of assets are required, and maintenance runs from the date of application.
- ***Chaturbhuj v. Sita Bai* (2008)**: the object of the provision is to prevent vagrancy.
- ***Badshah v. Urmila Badshah Godse* (2014)**: a second wife who did not know of the first marriage was granted maintenance.

**The 1986 Act in brief:**
- A divorced Muslim woman gets a reasonable provision and maintenance within iddat, her **mahr**, and her property.
- After iddat, if she cannot maintain herself, her **relatives** who would inherit from her pay, and failing them the **State Wakf Board**.

## MODULE 5: CHARGE, TRIAL AND RELATED PROCEDURES

### 1. Fair trial
A fair trial is the heart of criminal justice under Art. 21. Its elements are:
- **Presumption of innocence**, with proof beyond reasonable doubt (*Woolmington v. DPP*, 1935, the "golden thread"; *Kali Ram v. State of H.P.*, 1973).
- **Right to silence**: the accused cannot be compelled to testify, and no adverse comment may be made on silence (Art. 20(3); s.351; s.353).
- **Speedy trial** (*Hussainara Khatoon*; *P. Ramachandra Rao*, 2002).
- An **impartial judge**, an **open court**, a **lawyer** and **legal aid**.
- Evidence taken **in the presence of the accused**.
- The **right to cross-examine**.
- Supply of documents.
- Protection from **double jeopardy**.

*Zahira Habibullah Sheikh* (2004) explained that a fair trial is fair to the accused, the victim and society.

### 2. Limitation for taking cognizance (ss. 513–519)
To prevent stale prosecutions, cognizance is barred after:
- **6 months** for offences punishable with fine only;
- **1 year** for offences punishable with up to 1 year;
- **3 years** for offences punishable with 1 to 3 years.

There is **no limitation** for more serious offences.
- Time runs from the date of the offence or the date of knowledge (s.515).
- Certain periods are excluded (s.516).
- A **continuing offence** starts a fresh period every moment (s.518).
- The court may **condone delay** in the interest of justice (s.519).

In *Sarah Mathew* (2014), the Supreme Court held the relevant date is the date of **filing the complaint**, not the date of cognizance.

### 3. Charge (ss. 234–247)
A **charge** is the written accusation that tells the accused exactly what offence they must answer. Its purpose is **notice**, so that the accused can prepare a defence.

**Contents (ss. 234–235):** the offence, its name or definition, the section, and the particulars of **time, place, person or thing**. In criminal breach of trust, the gross sum over a period of up to one year may be stated.

**Errors and changes:**
- An error in the charge is **not fatal** unless the accused was **misled** and there was a **failure of justice** (s.238; *Willie Slaney v. State of M.P.*, 1956).
- The court may **alter or add to the charge** at any time before judgment, and witnesses may be recalled (ss.239–240).

**Joinder of charges.** The **basic rule** is that every distinct offence needs a **separate charge and a separate trial** (s.241). The exceptions are:
1. Offences of the **same kind within 12 months**, **up to five** of them under the BNSS (s.242).
2. Offences committed in the **same transaction** (s.243). The test is proximity of time, unity of place, unity of purpose, and continuity of action (*State of A.P. v. Cheemalapati Ganeswara Rao*, 1963).
3. **Doubt** about which offence the facts constitute: alternative charges may be framed (s.244).
4. Conviction for a **minor offence** included in the offence charged (s.245).
5. **Joint trial** of several accused involved in the same transaction (s.246).

### 4. Trial before a Court of Session (ss. 248–260)
The stages are:
1. The **Public Prosecutor opens** the case (ss.248–249).
2. **Discharge**: the accused may apply within 60 days. If there is no sufficient ground, the accused is discharged (s.250). At this stage the test is a **prima facie case / grave suspicion**, not proof (*Union of India v. Prafulla Kumar Samal*, 1979).
3. **Framing of charge** within 60 days of the first hearing (s.251).
4. The accused **pleads**. If they plead guilty, the court may convict (s.252).
5. **Prosecution evidence**: examination-in-chief, cross-examination and re-examination, possibly by video (s.254).
6. **Examination of the accused** under s.351 to explain the circumstances against them.
7. **Acquittal** if there is no evidence against the accused (s.255).
8. **Defence evidence** (s.256).
9. **Arguments** (s.257).
10. **Judgment** within 30 days of arguments, extendable to 45. If the accused is convicted, there is a **separate hearing on sentence** (s.258; *Santa Singh v. State of Punjab*, 1976).

### 5. Warrant case trials by magistrates (ss. 261–273)
**On a police report:**
1. Copies of documents are supplied.
2. Discharge if the charge is groundless (s.262).
3. Charge framed within 60 days (s.263).
4. Plea.
5. Prosecution evidence.
6. Defence evidence.

**Otherwise than on a police report** (complaint cases): the magistrate first takes **pre-charge evidence** (s.267), then discharges or frames the charge, and then the prosecution witnesses are recalled for cross-examination.

**Conclusion:** acquittal or conviction with a hearing on sentence (s.271). If the complainant is absent, discharge is possible in certain cases (s.272). For a **false accusation**, the court may order compensation (s.273).

### 6. Summons case trials (ss. 274–282)
- **No formal charge** is framed. The particulars of the offence are simply explained to the accused.
- The procedure is simpler and quicker.
- If the complainant is absent, the accused may be **acquitted** (s.279).
- The complainant may **withdraw** the complaint (s.280).
- The magistrate may **convert** a summons case into a warrant case (s.282).

### 7. Summary trials (ss. 283–288)
These are for **petty offences**, such as:
- offences punishable with up to 2 years;
- **theft of property worth up to ₹20,000**;
- house trespass;
- insult;
- criminal intimidation.

Under the BNSS, offences punishable with up to 3 years can also be tried summarily after hearing the accused. The procedure is that of summons cases. **Maximum sentence: 3 months.** Only a brief record and brief reasons are kept.

### 8. Evidence in inquiries and trials and commissions (ss. 307–336)
- Evidence is taken **in the presence of the accused or their lawyer**, now also by **video link** (s.308).
- A child victim of rape must not be confronted by the accused, though cross-examination is still ensured.
- Witnesses who are far away or important dignitaries can be examined on **commission** (ss.319–326).
- Reports of **Government scientific experts** can be used without calling them (s.329).
- Documents whose genuineness is **not disputed** need no formal proof; objection must be raised within 30 days (s.330).
- Under the new s.336, if a public servant, expert or investigating officer is transferred, retires or dies, their **successor** can depose.

### 9. Plea bargaining (ss. 289–300)
Plea bargaining means the accused **pleads guilty in return for a lighter sentence**. It was introduced in 2005.
- **Applies to** offences punishable with **up to 7 years**.
- **Does not apply** to offences against **women** or **children under 14**, or socio-economic offences.
- The accused must apply **within 30 days of the charge**. The court checks in private that it is **voluntary**.
- The parties work out a **mutually satisfactory disposition**, often with compensation to the victim.

**Sentence:**
- **half the minimum** sentence if a minimum is prescribed, otherwise **one-fourth** of the maximum;
- for **first-time offenders**, **one-fourth** of the minimum or **one-sixth** of the maximum.

The judgment is **final**, with no appeal except by writ or SLP. Statements made in a plea bargaining application cannot be used for any other purpose. Before 2005, the practice was held illegal (*State of U.P. v. Chandrika*, 2000).

### 10. Double jeopardy: autrefois acquit and autrefois convict (s.337 and Art. 20(2))
A person **tried and convicted or acquitted** by a competent court **cannot be tried again for the same offence**, or on the same facts for another offence that could have been charged.

**Exceptions:**
- a **distinct offence**, with State consent;
- **new consequences** that appear later, for example the victim of an assault dies afterwards;
- the first court was **not competent**.

A **discharge is not an acquittal**.

Article 20(2) protects only against **prosecution and punishment** twice. Section 337 is wider and covers acquittal too.

**Cases:**
- *Maqbool Hussain* (1953): customs proceedings are not "prosecution".
- *Thomas Dana* (1959): "same offence" means identical ingredients.
- *Kolla Veera Raghav Rao* (2011): a conviction under NI Act s.138 barred a later trial for cheating on the same facts.

### 11. Compounding and withdrawal (ss. 359, 360)
**Compounding (s.359):** the victim compromises with the accused.
- **Table 1** offences can be compounded **without the court's permission**.
- **Table 2** offences need the **court's permission**.
- Compounding has the effect of an **acquittal**.

Even **non-compoundable** offences can be **quashed by the High Court** on settlement in private disputes, but not in heinous offences (*Gian Singh*, 2012; *Narinder Singh*, 2014).

**Withdrawal from prosecution (s.360):** the PP may withdraw **with the court's consent** before judgment.
- Before the charge is framed, the effect is **discharge**. After, it is **acquittal**.
- The **victim must now be heard**.
- The PP must act **independently and in good faith**, not as a mouthpiece of the Government. The court checks that the withdrawal is in the public interest (*Sheonandan Paswan*, 1987; *State of Kerala v. K. Ajith*, 2021).

### 12. Irregular proceedings (ss. 506–512, 525–527)
The principle is that **procedural mistakes do not cancel proceedings unless they cause a failure of justice**.
- s.506 lists irregularities that **do not vitiate** proceedings, such as taking cognizance or issuing a search warrant by a magistrate not empowered, done in good faith.
- s.507 lists those that **do vitiate**, such as trying an offender, passing a sentence or deciding an appeal without power.
- Trial in the **wrong place** (s.508), an **error in the charge** (s.510) or an **error in sanction** (s.511) is not fatal unless justice failed.
- A judge must **not try a case in which they are personally interested** (s.525).

### 13. Inherent powers of the High Court (s.528, CrPC 482)
The High Court has **inherent powers** to:
- give effect to orders under the BNSS;
- **prevent abuse of the process of court**;
- **secure the ends of justice**.

These powers are used **sparingly**, mainly to **quash FIRs or proceedings**.

***State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal* (1992)** lists seven categories where quashing is proper:
1. the allegations do not make out an offence;
2. no cognizable offence is disclosed;
3. the uncontroverted allegations disclose no offence;
4. only a non-cognizable offence is disclosed;
5. the allegations are absurd;
6. there is a legal bar to the proceedings;
7. the proceedings are **mala fide**.

Other cases: *R.P. Kapur* (1960) and *Neeharika Infrastructure* (2021), which held the High Court should not pass blanket "no coercive steps" orders.

## MODULE 6: JUDGMENT, PROBATION, APPEALS, REVISION, EXECUTION

### 1. Judgment (ss. 392–406)
- A judgment is pronounced in **open court** and uploaded online within 7 days.
- It must state the **points for determination, the decision and the reasons** (s.393).
- **Special reasons** must be given for awarding **death** instead of life imprisonment.
- The court may order **compensation** to the victim (s.395; *Ankush Shivaji Gaikwad*, 2013, which said courts must consider compensation in every case).
- A **Victim Compensation Scheme** exists (s.396), and a new **Witness Protection Scheme** (s.398).
- Once signed, a judgment **cannot be altered** except to correct clerical or arithmetical errors (s.403).

### 2. Confirmation of death sentence (ss. 407–412)
A Sessions Court's death sentence **cannot be executed unless confirmed by the High Court**. The High Court may:
- take further evidence;
- confirm the sentence;
- reduce it;
- order a retrial;
- acquit.

A bench of **two judges** must sign the confirmation.

Death is given only in the "**rarest of rare**" cases (*Bachan Singh*, 1980), after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances (*Machhi Singh*, 1983). *Manoj v. State of M.P.* (2022) requires a mitigation investigation.

### 3. Probation and parole
**Probation** is a **reformative**, non-institutional treatment: the offender is released under supervision instead of being sent to prison. This avoids mixing first offenders with hardened criminals.

**Probation of Offenders Act, 1958:**
- **s.3**: release after **admonition** for minor offences (punishable with up to 2 years) by first offenders.
- **s.4**: release on **probation of good conduct** on a bond of up to 3 years, for offences not punishable with death or life.
- **s.5**: compensation to the victim.
- **s.6**: offenders **under 21** should not be imprisoned unless the court gives reasons.
- **s.12**: conviction carries **no disqualification**.
- **ss.13–14**: **Probation officers** inquire into the offender's background, report to the court, supervise the probationer, and help with rehabilitation and employment.

The **judiciary** has applied the Act liberally:
- *Rattan Lal v. State of Punjab* (1965): applied it retrospectively as a beneficial law.
- *Ishar Das v. State of Punjab* (1972): applied to a young offender.
- But where a statute fixes a **mandatory minimum**, probation is not possible (*State of M.P. v. Vikram Das*, 2019).

**Parole** is the **temporary or early release of a convict** who is already serving a sentence. It is granted by the **executive** under prison rules, for reasons such as family needs or good conduct. **Furlough** is a periodic release as an incentive for good conduct (*Asfaq v. State of Rajasthan*, 2017).

| Probation | Parole |
|---|---|
| Given **instead of** prison | Given **after** serving part of the sentence |
| By the **court** | By the **executive** |
| Under the Probation of Offenders Act / s.401 BNSS | Under prison rules |
| Judicial function | Administrative function |

### 4. Appeals (ss. 413–435)
- **No appeal lies unless the law provides one** (s.413). However, the **victim** can appeal against an acquittal, a conviction for a lesser offence, or inadequate compensation (*Mallikarjun Kodagali*, 2018).
- **Appeals against conviction** go:
  - from a Magistrate to the Sessions Court;
  - from a Sessions Court, or a Magistrate's sentence of more than 7 years, to the High Court;
  - from the High Court's original jurisdiction to the Supreme Court.
- There is **no appeal** if the accused **pleaded guilty** (except on the sentence), or in **petty cases** with very small sentences or fines.
- The State may appeal against an **inadequate sentence** (s.418).
- An appeal against **acquittal** needs the **High Court's leave** (s.419). A complainant needs **special leave** within 6 months (public servant) or 60 days (others).
- An acquittal strengthens the presumption of innocence, so it is reversed only if **perverse** (*Chandrappa v. State of Karnataka*, 2007).
- The appellate court may **suspend the sentence and grant bail** pending appeal (s.430).

### 5. Reference and revision (ss. 436–445)
**Reference (s.436):** when a subordinate court doubts the **validity of a law** on which the case depends, it must **refer** the question to the High Court.

**Revision (ss. 438, 442):** the High Court or Sessions Judge can **call for the record** to check the **correctness, legality or propriety** of an order.
- There is no revision against **interlocutory orders** (*Madhu Limaye v. State of Maharashtra*, 1977, explained intermediate orders).
- A party cannot file a **second revision** after one court has decided.
- A revision court **cannot convert an acquittal into a conviction**; it can only order a retrial.
- A revision is **not allowed where an appeal lay** but was not filed.

| Appeal | Revision |
|---|---|
| A **right** given by statute | A **discretion** of the court |
| Covers both facts and law | Covers legality and propriety only |
| Can reverse an acquittal | Cannot convert an acquittal into a conviction |

### 6. Transfer of cases (ss. 446–452)
- The **Supreme Court** can transfer cases **between States** for the ends of justice (s.446). In the Best Bakery case, the trial was moved from Gujarat to Maharashtra. The apprehension of an unfair trial must be **reasonable**, not imaginary (*Maneka Sanjay Gandhi v. Rani Jethmalani*, 1979).
- The **High Court** can transfer within the State if a fair trial is not possible, a difficult question of law arises, or for the convenience of parties and witnesses (s.447).
- The **Sessions Judge** can transfer within the sessions division (s.448).

### 7. Execution, suspension, remission and commutation (ss. 453–477)
- Sentences are executed by **warrants**.
- A **pregnant woman's** death sentence **must be commuted** to life imprisonment (s.455).
- Detention during investigation and trial is **set off** against the sentence (s.468).
- A new **mercy petition** procedure applies to death row convicts (s.472).
- The Government may **suspend or remit** a sentence (s.473) and **commute** it (s.474): death to another punishment, life imprisonment to not less than 7 years, and so on.
- A person serving life for an offence punishable with death **cannot be released before 14 years** (s.475).
- In certain cases the State must act with the Centre's **concurrence** (s.477; *Union of India v. V. Sriharan*, 2016).

These powers are separate from the **constitutional pardon powers** of the President (Art. 72) and Governor (Art. 161), which are subject to limited judicial review (*Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006). Unexplained **delay in deciding a mercy petition** can justify commutation (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014).

### 8. Tender of pardon (ss. 343–345)
To obtain evidence against the main offenders, a magistrate may **pardon an accomplice** on condition of a **full and true disclosure**. This applies to Sessions-triable offences or offences punishable with 7 years or more.
- The pardoned person becomes an **approver** and is examined as a witness.
- The approver is kept in custody until the trial ends.
- After commitment, the trial court may also tender a pardon (s.344).
- If the approver **breaks the condition**, they can be tried for the offence (s.345).

The approver's evidence needs **corroboration** (*Suresh Chandra Bahri*, 1995).

### 9. Accused persons of unsound mind (ss. 367–378)
If the accused appears to be of unsound mind and **incapable of making a defence**:
- the court gets a medical opinion;
- **postpones** the inquiry or trial;
- may **release them on security** or send them to a care facility.

The trial resumes when they recover.

If the accused was of unsound mind **at the time of the act** (BNS s.22), they are **acquitted**, but the court must record whether they did the act. They are then kept in **safe custody** or handed to a relative (ss.373–374).

The accused must prove insanity on a **preponderance of probabilities** (*Dahyabhai*, 1964).

## MODULE 7: JUVENILE JUSTICE
*(The syllabus title mentions the 1986 Act, but the content covers the JJ (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.)*

### 1. Concept and history
Juvenile justice is based on the idea that **children are not small adults**. Their offences come from immaturity and circumstances, so the law aims at **reformation, rehabilitation and the best interests of the child**, not punishment. This rests on the doctrine of ***parens patriae***: the State acts as guardian of the child.

**History:**
- Apprentices Act, 1850.
- Reformatory Schools Act, 1897.
- Provincial Children Acts (Madras 1920, Bengal 1922, Bombay 1924).
- Children Act, 1960.
- *Sheela Barse v. UOI* (1986) led to the first uniform law, the **JJ Act, 1986** (juvenile age: 16 for boys, 18 for girls).
- India ratified the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** in 1992.
- **JJ Act, 2000**: the age was made 18 for all.
- After the **Nirbhaya case (2012)**, the **JJ Act, 2015** allowed **16–18 year-olds accused of heinous offences** to be tried as adults after an assessment.
- **2021 amendment**: gave a bigger role to **District Magistrates**.

**International standards:** the UNCRC, the **Beijing Rules** (1985), the **Riyadh Guidelines** and the **Havana Rules** (1990).

**BNS provisions:**
- **s.20**: a child **under 7** cannot commit any offence. This is complete immunity.
- **s.21**: a child **aged 7 to 12** is not liable if they lack the **maturity to understand** the nature and consequences of the act. This is partial immunity.

### 2. Child in conflict with law vs child in need of care and protection
- A **child in conflict with law (CCL)** is a child under 18 **alleged or found to have committed an offence**.
- A **child in need of care and protection (CNCP)** is a child who is a **victim of circumstances**. Examples:
  - homeless, begging or working illegally;
  - abused or neglected;
  - orphaned, abandoned or surrendered;
  - missing or runaway;
  - trafficked or at risk of child marriage;
  - affected by disaster or conflict;
  - with parents unfit to care for them.

| CCL | CNCP |
|---|---|
| Handled by the **Juvenile Justice Board** | Handled by the **Child Welfare Committee** |
| Placed in observation or special homes | Placed in children's homes, foster care or adoption |
| Focus: reformation | Focus: care and protection |

### 3. Juvenile Justice Board and Children's Court
The **JJB** consists of a **Principal Magistrate** (a JMFC) and **two social workers**, one of whom must be a woman.

**Key rules:**
- A child is **never placed in a police lock-up or jail**, and is produced before the JJB within 24 hours.
- **Bail is the norm** (s.12). It is refused only if release would expose the child to danger or criminals, or defeat the ends of justice.
- The inquiry must finish in **4 months**.

**Offences are classed as:**
- **petty**: punishable with up to 3 years;
- **serious**: 3 to 7 years;
- **heinous**: a **minimum of 7 years** or more.

*Shilpa Mittal* (2020) held that an offence with no minimum sentence is "serious", not "heinous".

For a heinous offence by a 16–18 year-old, the JJB makes a **preliminary assessment** within 3 months of the child's mental and physical capacity and understanding. It may then send the case to the **Children's Court** (a Sessions Court) for trial as an adult (s.15).

**Orders the JJB can make:** admonition, counselling, community service, fine, probation, or placement in a **special home** for up to **3 years**.

**No child gets a death sentence or life imprisonment without release.**

**Cases:**
- *Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand* (2005): age is determined on the **date of the offence**.
- *Salil Bali* (2013) and *Subramanian Swamy v. Raju* (2014): upheld the scheme.
- *Jitendra Singh* (2013): a claim of juvenility can be raised **at any stage**.

### 4. Child Welfare Committee, SJPU and Childline
**Child Welfare Committee (CWC):**
- A **chairperson and four members**, including a woman and a child expert, in every district.
- It has the powers of a magistrate.
- **Functions:**
  - receive children produced before it, within 24 hours;
  - inquire into their situation and order social investigation reports;
  - decide placement (restoring the child to family, children's home, fit person, foster care);
  - declare children **legally free for adoption**;
  - inspect institutions;
  - ensure rehabilitation.
- The inquiry should be completed in 4 months.

**Special Juvenile Police Unit (SJPU):** each police station has a **Child Welfare Police Officer**. Each district has an SJPU led by a DSP-rank officer with two social workers. They handle children sensitively: in plain clothes, with no handcuffs and no lock-up.

**Childline (1098):** a 24-hour emergency helpline that rescues children in distress and brings them before the CWC. It is now linked with the 112 emergency system under Mission Vatsalya.

**Rehabilitation** is done through family-based care where possible:
- **adoption**;
- **foster care** (s.44);
- **sponsorship** (financial support to the family, s.45);
- **after-care** for young people leaving institutions at 18, until age 21 (s.46).

### 5. Institutions
| Institution | For whom |
|---|---|
| **Observation Home** (s.47) | CCL, temporarily, during the inquiry |
| **Special Home** (s.48) | CCL found to have committed an offence, for rehabilitation |
| **Place of Safety** (s.49) | Children aged 16–18 accused or convicted of heinous offences, and persons over 18 |
| **Children's Home** (s.50) | CNCP, for long-term care, education and training |
| **Open Shelter** (s.43) | Short-term support |
| **Fit Facility** (s.51) | A recognised organisation that temporarily takes responsibility for a child |
| **Fit Person** (s.52) | A recognised person who temporarily takes responsibility for a child |

- **Registration** of all child care institutions is compulsory.
- Standards of food, health, education and recreation are fixed by rules.
- **Inspection committees** visit regularly, and there are **social audits**.
- The **NCPCR / SCPCR** monitor implementation.
- *Sampurna Behrua v. UOI* (2018) pushed for proper implementation.

### 6. Adoption under the JJ Act; CARA; inter-country adoption
The JJ Act provides a **secular adoption law** for orphaned, abandoned and surrendered children. Any person of any religion can adopt (*Shabnam Hashmi v. UOI*, 2014), unlike the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, which applies only to Hindus.

**Steps:**
1. The CWC declares the child **legally free for adoption**. Surrendering parents get **60 days** to reconsider.
2. Prospective adoptive parents register on **CARINGS** (the online portal).
3. A **home study** is done.
4. The child is matched and placed in pre-adoption foster care.
5. The **District Magistrate passes the adoption order** (since 2021; earlier this was the court).

**Eligibility of adoptive parents:**
- physically, mentally and financially fit;
- a couple needs both spouses' consent and **2 years of a stable marriage**;
- a **single woman** may adopt a child of any gender;
- a **single man cannot adopt a girl**;
- the age gap between child and parent must be at least **25 years**.

**Effect (s.63):** the child becomes the adoptive parents' child **for all purposes, including inheritance**. Ties with the birth family end. Adopting outside this procedure is punishable (s.80).

**CARA (Central Adoption Resource Authority, s.68):** a statutory body under the Ministry of Women and Child Development. It:
- is India's **Central Authority under the Hague Convention, 1993**;
- **frames adoption regulations** (the Adoption Regulations, 2022);
- promotes in-country adoption;
- regulates inter-country adoption and issues the **NOC** for it;
- supervises the State and specialised adoption agencies;
- runs CARINGS.

**Inter-country adoption (s.59):**
- Allowed only if no Indian family adopts the child within **60 days**.
- **Preference order:** NRIs, then OCIs, then foreigners.
- Applications come through an **Authorised Foreign Adoption Agency**.
- CARA issues the NOC and the DM passes the order.
- There is **post-adoption follow-up for 2 years**.

***Lakshmi Kant Pandey v. UOI* (1984)** laid down the original safeguards against trafficking: priority to Indian families, recognised agencies only, home studies, judicial scrutiny, follow-up reports, and no commercial element. The guiding principle throughout is the **best interests of the child**.
