# INTERPRETATION OF STATUTES (LCC 0703): Module-wise Explanation

## MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO INTERPRETATION AND THE GENERAL CLAUSES ACT, 1897

### 1. Meaning, need and object of interpretation
**Meaning.** Interpretation is the process by which courts find out the **meaning and intention of the legislature** from the words it has used in a statute. Salmond describes it as "the process by which the courts seek to ascertain the meaning of the legislature through the medium of authoritative forms in which it is expressed."

The legislature speaks only through the text. Once the law is passed, the courts have to apply that text to real disputes. This is where interpretation comes in.

**Need.**
1. **Language is imperfect.** Words often have more than one meaning.
2. **Drafting has limits.** Legislators cannot foresee every situation.
3. **Society changes.** New technology and new social conditions arise after a law is made. For example, the internet and electronic records did not exist when the Evidence Act was drafted.
4. **General words** must be applied to specific facts.
5. **Provisions can conflict**, either within one Act or between two Acts.
6. **Complex statutes** must be read as a whole for the parts to make sense.

**Object.** The main object is to **give effect to the intention of the legislature**. Related aims are:
- to make the law **workable**, following the maxim *ut res magis valeat quam pereat* (it is better for a thing to have effect than to fail);
- to **resolve ambiguity** and fill small gaps;
- to ensure **certainty and justice**.

*R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. UOI* (1957): the intention of the legislature must be gathered from the words used, read in their context.

### 2. Interpretation vs construction
Writers draw a distinction between the two terms, though courts use them interchangeably.

- **Interpretation** means finding the **meaning of the words**. It is **linguistic** and stays close to the text.
- **Construction** means drawing **conclusions beyond the direct expression of the text**, from its spirit and purpose. It is used when the words are unclear or a situation is not covered.

**Cooley:** "Interpretation is the art of finding out the true sense of any form of words... Construction is the drawing of conclusions respecting subjects that lie beyond the direct expression of the text."

| Interpretation | Construction |
|---|---|
| Finds the meaning of words | Draws legal conclusions beyond the words |
| Linguistic | Legal |
| Used when words are clear | Used when words are ambiguous or silent |
| Literal sense | Spirit and purpose |

### 3. Concept and classification of statutes
A **statute** is written law made by a **competent legislature**. It includes Acts of Parliament and State legislatures, Ordinances, and delegated legislation (rules, regulations, bye-laws).

Statutes are classified in several ways.

**By duration**
- **Perpetual**: no fixed time limit. Stays in force until repealed.
- **Temporary**: lasts for a fixed period and then expires.

**By method**
- **Mandatory**: must be strictly obeyed. Non-compliance makes the act invalid.
- **Directory**: substantial compliance is enough.
- How to tell them apart: look at the **intention** of the legislature, the **object** of the provision, and the **consequences of non-compliance**. The word "shall" is not decisive by itself. See *State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* (1957); *Sharif-ud-Din v. Abdul Gani Lone* (1980).

**By object**
- **Codifying**: states the whole law on a subject.
- **Consolidating**: gathers existing laws into one Act.
- **Declaratory**: removes doubts about what the law is.
- **Remedial**: gives a new remedy or benefit.
- **Amending**: changes an existing law.
- **Repealing**: ends an existing law.
- **Penal**: imposes punishment.
- **Taxing**: imposes tax.
- **Enabling**: grants powers.
- **Disabling**: restricts rights.
- **Validating**: cures past defects.

**By extent**
- **General**: applies to the whole community.
- **Local, special or private**: applies to a place, a subject, or particular persons.

**By operation in time**
- **Prospective** or **retrospective**.

### 4. General Clauses Act, 1897
**Why it exists.** If every Act had to define common words like "person", "month" or "good faith", and repeat general rules about repeal and time, statutes would be very long and inconsistent. The GCA provides **common definitions and general rules** that apply to all Central Acts and Regulations, **unless there is something repugnant in the subject or context**. States have their own General Clauses Acts. Article 367 applies the GCA to the interpretation of the **Constitution** as well.

**Preliminary (ss. 1–2):** short title, extent and repeal.

**Definitions (s.3).** Important ones:
- "**act**" includes illegal omissions;
- "**document**";
- "**good faith**": a thing done honestly, **whether negligently or not**;
- "**immovable property**" and "**movable property**";
- "**month**": a British calendar month;
- "**offence**";
- "**person**": includes any company or association, incorporated or not;
- "**sign**";
- "**State**";
- "**writing**".

**General rules of construction**
- **s.5 (commencement):** if no date is given, a Central Act comes into force on the **day it receives assent**. It takes effect from the **midnight** at the start of that day.
- **s.6 (effect of repeal):** a repeal does not revive anything, does not affect past operation, accrued rights and liabilities, penalties already incurred, or pending proceedings, unless a different intention appears. (Covered in detail in Module 7.)
- **s.6A:** repealing an amending Act does not undo the amendments it already made.
- **s.7:** a repealed Act is revived only if this is expressly stated.
- **s.8:** references to a repealed provision are read as references to the re-enacted provision.
- **s.9 (time):** "from" **excludes** the first day; "to" **includes** the last day.
- **s.10:** if the last day for doing something falls when the **court or office is closed**, it may be done on the **next working day**.
- **s.11:** distances are measured in a straight line on a horizontal plane.
- **s.13 (gender and number):** the **masculine includes the feminine**, and the **singular includes the plural** and vice versa.

**Powers and functionaries**
- **s.14:** a power may be **exercised from time to time** as needed.
- **s.15:** a power to appoint includes a power to appoint ex officio.
- **s.16:** a **power to appoint includes a power to suspend or dismiss**.
- **s.17:** references to an office cover whoever holds it.
- **s.18:** references cover successors.
- **s.19:** references to official chiefs cover their subordinates.

**Orders, rules and bye-laws made under enactments**
- **s.20:** words in rules and notifications have the **same meaning as in the parent Act**.
- **s.21:** the **power to issue** a notification, order or rule **includes the power to add to, amend, vary or rescind** it. This does not apply to quasi-judicial orders (*Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare*, 2002).
- **s.23:** rules may be made only after **previous publication** where the Act requires it.
- **s.24:** rules and orders made under a repealed Act **continue** under the re-enacted Act, so far as they are consistent with it.

**Miscellaneous**
- **s.26:** if an act is an offence under **two enactments**, the offender may be prosecuted under either, but **not punished twice**.
- **s.27:** service by **registered post**, properly addressed, is deemed to be effected.

## MODULE 2: GENERAL PRINCIPLES AND RULES OF INTERPRETATION

### 1. Mimansa principles of interpretation (Indian Knowledge System)
**Background.** Mimansa is an ancient Indian school of interpretation founded by **Jaimini** (the *Mimansa Sutras*). It was developed further by **Shabara, Kumarila Bhatta and Prabhakara**. It was originally created to interpret the **Vedas** so that rituals could be performed correctly. Later, jurists such as **Vijnaneshwara** (author of the *Mitakshara*) and **Jimutavahana** used it to interpret the Smritis, which was the law of the time.

It is a highly **rational and systematic** method. In some ways it is more refined than the English rules.

**Modern revival.** Justice **Markandey Katju** brought Mimansa back into Indian law through his article "The Mimansa Principles of Interpretation" (1993) 1 SCC (Jour) 16, and through judgments such as:
- ***Ispat Industries Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs* (2006)**;
- ***Udai Shankar Awasthi v. State of U.P.* (2013)**.

**The six tests for finding the meaning of a text (shadlinga)**
1. **Upakrama–Upasamhara**: the beginning and the conclusion of the text must be read together.
2. **Abhyasa**: repetition shows emphasis.
3. **Apurvata**: what is new or unique in the text.
4. **Phala**: the result or purpose.
5. **Arthavada**: explanatory or praising passages.
6. **Upapatti**: logical reasoning and consistency.

**The six means of interpretation, in decreasing order of strength**
1. **Shruti**: the **express words**. This is like the literal rule.
2. **Linga**: the **suggestive power** of words, i.e. the implied meaning.
3. **Vakya**: the **syntactical connection**, i.e. the sentence as a whole.
4. **Prakarana**: the **context**.
5. **Sthana**: the **position** or order of the words.
6. **Samakhya**: the **name** or etymology.

When these conflict, the **earlier one prevails** over the later.

**Important axioms (nyayas)**
- **Sarthakya**: every word must have a meaning. No word is redundant. This is the same as the modern rule against surplusage.
- **Laghava**: prefer the **simpler** interpretation that needs fewer assumptions.
- **Arthaikatva**: a word has **one meaning in one context**. Double meanings are avoided.
- **Gunapradhan**: when a **subordinate (guna)** provision conflicts with the **principal (pradhan)** provision, the subordinate gives way. In *Ispat Industries*, the Customs Valuation Rules (subordinate) gave way to the Customs Act (principal).
- **Samanjasya**: reconcile apparently conflicting provisions. This is the same idea as **harmonious construction**.
- **Vikalpa**: when two provisions genuinely cannot be reconciled, an **option** is allowed. This is used only as a last resort.
- **Atidesha**: extending the features of one thing to another by **analogy**.
- **Badha**: one rule is excluded in favour of another.

**Vidhi, Nishedha and Arthavada.** Mimansa divides texts into:
- **Vidhi**: a positive command;
- **Nishedha**: a prohibition;
- **Arthavada**: explanatory or recommendatory statements.

Arthavada is **directory**, not binding. Courts have used this idea to tell **mandatory** provisions from **directory** ones.

### 2. Literal rule
**Rule.** Words must be given their **plain, ordinary and grammatical meaning**. If the words are clear, the court must apply them **even if the result is harsh**, because the legislature is presumed to mean what it says.

**Sussex Peerage Case** (1844), Tindal C.J.: if the words are precise and unambiguous, nothing more is needed than to expound them in their natural and ordinary sense.

**Cases**
- ***Whiteley v. Chappell* (1868)**: it was an offence to impersonate "any person entitled to vote". The accused impersonated a **dead voter**. A dead person is not "entitled to vote", so the accused was **acquitted**. This shows how the literal rule can produce absurd results.
- *R v. Harris* (1836): the words were "stab, cut or wound". **Biting off a nose** did not fall within them.
- *Fisher v. Bell* (1961): displaying a flick-knife in a shop window was an **invitation to treat**, not an "**offer for sale**".
- ***Nelson Motis v. UOI* (1992)**: clear words must be applied as they are.
- *Kanai Lal Sur v. Paramnidhi Sadhukhan* (1957): if the words are clear, the consequences are irrelevant.
- ***Raghunath Rai Bareja v. Punjab National Bank* (2007)**: the literal rule is the **first rule** of interpretation. A hard case does not justify departing from it.
- *Ramavtar Budhaiprasad v. ATO* (1961): words in a taxing statute are read in their **popular sense**. "Vegetables" did **not include betel leaves**.

**Merits**
- It respects the **separation of powers**.
- It gives **certainty**.
- It prevents judges from making law.

**Demerits**
- It can produce **absurd or unjust** results.
- It ignores the **purpose** of the law.
- It assumes language is perfect, which it is not.

### 3. Golden rule
**Rule.** A modified literal rule. The ordinary meaning is followed **unless** it leads to **absurdity, inconsistency, hardship or repugnancy**. In that case the meaning may be **modified, but only as far as necessary** to avoid the absurdity.

Lord Wensleydale in ***Grey v. Pearson* (1857)**: "the grammatical and ordinary sense of the words is to be adhered to unless that would lead to some absurdity... in which case [it] may be modified so as to avoid that absurdity... but no further."

**Cases**
- ***R v. Allen* (1872)**: bigamy was defined as "whoever being married shall **marry** another". A second marriage is legally void, so literally no one could ever "marry" a second time. "Marry" was read as "**go through a form of marriage**".
- ***Re Sigsworth* (1935)**: a son who **murdered his mother** was not allowed to inherit from her, even though the succession statute literally entitled him to.
- *Adler v. George* (1964): "**in the vicinity of**" a prohibited place was read to include "**in**" the place.
- ***Tirath Singh v. Bachittar Singh* (1955)**: a provision requiring notice "to persons" was read narrowly to avoid the absurdity of having to give notice to everyone.
- ***State of Punjab v. Qaisar Jehan Begum* (1963)**: the "**date of the award**" meant the date the party **came to know** of it, not the date it was signed.

**Merit:** it avoids absurd results while keeping close to the text.
**Demerit:** what counts as "absurd" is **subjective**.

### 4. Mischief rule (Heydon's rule)
**Origin.** ***Heydon's Case* (1584).** This is the **oldest** of the rules. The court must consider four things:
1. What was the **law before** the Act?
2. What was the **mischief or defect** the old law did not cover?
3. What **remedy** has Parliament provided?
4. What is the **true reason** for the remedy?

The court must then adopt the meaning that **suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy**.

**Cases**
- ***Smith v. Hughes* (1960)**: prostitutes were charged with soliciting "**in a street**". They had solicited from **balconies and windows**. They were held liable, because the mischief was the harassment of people in the street.
- *Corkery v. Carpenter* (1951): a **bicycle** was held to be a "carriage".
- ***Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar* (1955)**: Art. 286 was interpreted to prevent multiple taxation of inter-State sales.
- ***Kanwar Singh v. Delhi Administration* (1965)**: "**owner**" of stray cattle included a person in **possession or control** of them.
- *Alamgir v. State of Bihar* (1959).
- *Sodra Devi v. CIT* (1957): "individual" included **women**.

**Modern form.** The mischief rule has developed into the broader **purposive approach**: read the statute to carry out its purpose (*Pepper v. Hart*, 1993).

**Merits:** it gives effect to legislative purpose and avoids injustice.
**Demerits:** judges get wide discretion, and finding the original "mischief" in old records can be difficult.

### 5. Harmonious construction
**Rule.** When two provisions in the same statute, or in two statutes, appear to **conflict**, the court must try to **reconcile them** so that **both have effect**. One provision must not be read in a way that makes the other **useless**. The statute is read **as a whole**.

**Principles from *CIT v. Hindustan Bulk Carriers* (2003)**
- Avoid a **head-on clash** between provisions.
- One provision cannot be used to **defeat** another unless reconciliation is impossible.
- If they cannot be fully reconciled, give effect to **both as far as possible**.
- Neither provision should become a **dead letter**.

**Cases**
- ***Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore* (1958)**: Art. 25(2)(b) (throwing open Hindu temples to all classes) appeared to conflict with Art. 26(b) (a denomination's right to manage its religious affairs). They were harmonised: the temple was open to all, but the denomination could **exclude the public from certain special ceremonies**.
- ***Re Kerala Education Bill* (1957)**: Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles should be harmonised.
- *Sri Shankari Prasad v. UOI* (1951): Articles 13 and 368 were harmonised.
- *M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha* (1959): Art. 19(1)(a) and legislative privileges were harmonised.

This rule is the modern equivalent of Mimansa's **Samanjasya** principle.

### 6. Rule of last antecedent
**Rule.** Qualifying words or phrases normally refer to the **nearest preceding word** (the last antecedent), and not to all the earlier words, **unless the context shows otherwise**.

**Example.** In "a dog, a horse or a cat that is black", "that is black" qualifies only "cat".

**Exception.** If a **comma** separates the qualifying phrase from all the antecedents, or the sense requires it, the phrase may apply to **all of them**.

Indian courts treat this rule only as an **aid**. It gives way to the **context and intention** of the statute (*Lockhart v. United States*, 2016, is a modern US example of the rule).

### 7. Non-obstante clause
**Meaning.** A clause beginning "**Notwithstanding anything contained in...**". It gives the provision **overriding effect** over the provisions or laws it names, but **only where there is a conflict**.

**Principles**
- If there is **no conflict**, the clause has no work to do.
- It must be read together with the **enacting part** of the section. It does not cut down the enacting part.
  - *Aswini Kumar Ghosh v. Arabinda Bose* (1952);
  - ***Chandavarkar Sita Ratna Rao v. Ashalata Guram* (1986)**;
  - *R.S. Raghunath v. State of Karnataka* (1992).
- When **two statutes both contain non-obstante clauses**, the courts usually prefer the **later** statute or the **special** one, after looking at the purpose of each (*Solidaire India v. Fairgrowth Financial Services*, 2001).

**Examples:** IBC s.238; Art. 31C of the Constitution.

### 8. Legal fiction
**Meaning.** The law **deems** something to exist, or treats one thing as another, even though in reality it is not so. Typical words are "**deemed**", "**as if**", and "**shall be treated as**".

**Principle.** Lord Asquith in ***East End Dwellings Co. v. Finsbury Borough Council* (1952)**: if you are told to imagine a state of affairs as real, you must also imagine as real all its **inevitable consequences**. "**Your imagination must not boggle**" at the corollaries.

This was adopted in India in ***State of Bombay v. Pandurang Vinayak* (1953)**.

**Limit.** A legal fiction must be **confined to the purpose** for which it was created. It cannot be extended beyond that purpose (*Bengal Immunity*, 1955; *CIT v. Amarchand Shroff*, 1963).

**Examples:**
- a company treated as a legal person;
- an adopted child treated as a natural child;
- the deemed partition under s.6 of the Hindu Succession Act.

### 9. Conjunctive and disjunctive words: "and" / "or"
**Ordinary meaning**
- "**And**" is **conjunctive**: all the conditions must be satisfied.
- "**Or**" is **disjunctive**: any one of the alternatives is enough.

**Exception.** If the ordinary reading would cause **absurdity** or defeat the clear **intention** of the legislature, the court may read "and" as "or", or "or" as "and".

- *R v. Oakes* (1959): "**and**" was read as "**or**" to avoid an absurd result.
- *Joint Director of Mines Safety v. Tandur & Nayandgi Stone Quarries* (1987) is also cited on this point.

Courts do this **only when necessary**, as an application of the golden rule.

### 10. Construction of general words
**(a) Ejusdem generis ("of the same kind")**

**Rule.** When **general words follow a list of specific words** that form a single class (genus), the general words are **limited to things of the same class**.

**Example.** In "dogs, cats and other animals", "other animals" means other **domestic** animals, not lions or tigers.

**Conditions (*Amar Chandra Chakraborty v. Collector of Excise*, 1972)**
1. There is a **list of specific words**.
2. The specific words form a **distinct class or genus**.
3. The class is **not exhausted** by the list.
4. **General words follow** the list.
5. There is **no contrary intention** in the statute.

**Cases**
- ***Powell v. Kempton Park Racecourse* (1899)**: "house, office, room or **other place**" used for betting. The specific words described **indoor places**, so an **open-air enclosure** was not covered.
- *Evans v. Cross* (1938): painted white lines on a road were not a "**traffic sign**".
- *Kochuni v. State of Madras* (1960).
- *U.P. State Electricity Board v. Hari Shankar Jain* (1978).
- *Lilavati Bai v. State of Bombay* (1957): the rule was **not applied**, because the legislature clearly intended a wider meaning.

**(b) Noscitur a sociis ("a word is known by the company it keeps")**

**Rule.** The meaning of an unclear word is found by looking at the **words around it**. This is **wider** than ejusdem generis; ejusdem generis is really one application of it.

**Cases**
- *Foster v. Diphwys Casson* (1887): "case or canister" for carrying explosives did **not include a cloth bag**.
- *Pengelly v. Bell Punch Co.* (1964): "floors, steps, stairs, passages" meant floors used for **passage**, not for storage.
- ***State of Bombay v. Hospital Mazdoor Sabha* (1960)**: the court did not narrow "**industry**", and a hospital was held to be an industry.
- ***Rainbow Steels v. CST* (1981)**: "old, discarded, unserviceable or obsolete machinery". "Old" took its colour from the other words and meant **no longer usable**.
- *Godfrey Phillips v. State of U.P.* (2005): "**luxuries**", read with "entertainment, amusement, betting", did **not include tobacco**.

**(c) Reddendo singula singulis ("referring each to each")**

**Rule.** Where a sentence has **several subjects and several objects**, each is matched with its **appropriate** partner (read distributively).

**Example.** "If anyone shall **draw or load** any **sword or gun**" is read as "**draw a sword** or **load a gun**". One does not "load" a sword.

**(d) Words of rank**

**Rule.** When general words follow words of a **particular rank**, they cover only persons or things of **equal or lower rank**, never higher.

**Examples**
- "Deans, archdeacons, parsons and **others having spiritual promotion**" did **not include bishops**, who rank higher.
- "Earls, barons and **other nobles**" did **not include dukes**.

**Related maxims**
- ***Expressio unius est exclusio alterius***: express mention of one thing excludes others.
- ***Generalia specialibus non derogant***: a general provision does not override a special one.

## MODULE 3: INTERNAL AND EXTERNAL AIDS TO INTERPRETATION

When the words of a statute are unclear, the court looks for help. **Internal aids** are found **within the statute**. **External aids** come from **outside** it.

### 1. Internal aids

**(a) Long title and short title**
- The **long title** describes the purpose of the Act. It may be used to resolve ambiguity.
- The **short title** is only a label for identification.

**(b) Preamble**
The preamble sets out the **object and reasons** for the Act. It has been called the "**key to open the mind of the legislature**".

- It can be used **only where the enacting words are ambiguous**. It **cannot override** clear words (*A.G. v. Prince Ernest Augustus of Hanover*, 1957; *Burrakur Coal Co. v. UOI*, 1961).
- For the Constitution: ***Re Berubari Union* (1960)** held the Preamble was **not part** of the Constitution. ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** overruled this and held that the Preamble **is part of the Constitution** and an important aid to interpreting it.

**(c) Definition clauses**
- "**Means**": the definition is **exhaustive**. Nothing else is included.
- "**Includes**": the definition is **extensive**. It adds to the ordinary meaning.
- "**Means and includes**": the definition is **exhaustive** (*P. Kasilingam v. PSG College of Technology*, 1995).
- Definitions apply "**unless the context otherwise requires**".

**(d) Headings**
Headings over a section or group of sections act like a **preamble** to them. They can help **resolve doubt**, but they **cannot control the clear meaning** of the section (***Bhinka v. Charan Singh*, 1959**; *Frick India v. UOI*, 1990).

**(e) Marginal notes**
These are short notes printed beside a section, usually added by the draftsman and not voted on by the legislature. So they carry **little weight**. In the **Constitution**, however, the marginal notes were adopted by the Constituent Assembly, so they are given more weight (*Bengal Immunity*, 1955).

**(f) Illustrations**
Illustrations (as in the BNS and the Evidence Act) show how a section works. They **cannot restrict or expand** the section. If an illustration conflicts with the section, the **section prevails** (***Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer*, 1956**).

**(g) Proviso**
A proviso ("**Provided that...**") normally **makes an exception to, or qualifies,** the main provision. It does not usually expand it.

***S. Sundaram Pillai v. V.R. Pattabiraman* (1985)** listed four roles of a proviso:
1. to **qualify or make an exception** to the main provision;
2. to **change the concept** of the main provision by adding mandatory conditions;
3. to become an **integral part** of the provision, taking on the character of a substantive enactment;
4. to act as an **optional addendum** that only explains the provision.

See also *Shah Bhojraj Kuverji Oil Mills v. Subhash Chandra Yograj Sinha* (1961).

**(h) Explanation**
An explanation **clarifies** the meaning of the main provision, removes doubts, or fills a gap. It does not normally enlarge the section. An explanation added "**for removal of doubts**" can, however, widen its scope. *S. Sundaram Pillai* (1985) also set out the objects of an explanation.

**(i) Schedule**
A schedule is the **appendix** to an Act (forms, lists, details). It is **part of the statute** and is read with it. If the schedule **conflicts** with the main body of the Act, the **main body prevails** (***Aphali Pharmaceuticals v. State of Maharashtra*, 1989**).

**(j) Punctuation**
Older courts gave punctuation **little weight**, because it was not enacted (*Aswini Kumar Ghosh*, 1952). In modern, carefully drafted statutes, punctuation **can matter** (*Mohd. Shabbir v. State of Maharashtra*, 1979, where a comma affected the meaning).

**(k) The statute as a whole**
Under the maxim ***ex visceribus actus***, the statute is construed from within itself, reading all its parts together.

### 2. External aids

**(a) Parliamentary history: debates and proceedings in the legislature**
This includes speeches made while a Bill was debated, the **Statement of Objects and Reasons**, committee reports, and amendments that were proposed.

**English position**
- The traditional **exclusionary rule** said courts could not look at Parliamentary debates.
- ***Pepper v. Hart* (1993)** relaxed this. **Hansard** (the record of debates) may be used where:
  1. the law is **ambiguous**, obscure, or leads to absurdity;
  2. the statement relied on was made by a **Minister or the promoter** of the Bill;
  3. the statement is **clear**.

**Indian position**
- Earlier cases did **not** allow debates to be used (*Aswini Kumar Ghosh*, 1952).
- The **Statement of Objects and Reasons** may be used to understand the **background and the mischief**, but **not** to control the meaning of the words (*State of W.B. v. Subodh Gopal Bose*, 1954).
- ***K.P. Varghese v. ITO* (1981)**: the **Finance Minister's speech** introducing the Bill was used to interpret s.52(2) of the Income Tax Act.
- ***R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay* (1984)**: reports of committees and the Law Commission may be used to find the mischief. Parliamentary history may also be used where the words are ambiguous.
- *Kesavananda Bharati* and *Indra Sawhney* referred to the **Constituent Assembly Debates**.

**Present position:** Parliamentary history is a **useful aid** for understanding context and purpose, but it is **not conclusive**.

**(b) Socio-economic and technological developments**
A statute is "**always speaking**". It should be read in the light of **present conditions** (this is called **updating** or dynamic construction).

- ***State of Punjab v. Amritsar Beverages* (2006)**: "**books of account**" included **computer hard discs**.
- *Senior Electric Inspector v. Laxminarayan Chopra* (1962): "telegraph line" included **wireless**.
- *National Textile Workers' Union v. P.R. Ramakrishnan* (1983): law cannot stand still while society changes.

**(c) Reference to other statutes**
- **Statutes in pari materia** (on the **same subject**) are read **together** as one system (*State of Madras v. Vaidyanatha Iyer*, 1958).
- **Earlier and repealed statutes** show the mischief the new law was meant to address.
- **Later statutes** may explain an earlier ambiguous one.
- Definitions borrowed from **unrelated** statutes are used **cautiously**.

**(d) International conventions**
Where domestic law is **silent or ambiguous**, courts may use **international conventions** India has signed, as long as they are **consistent with domestic law** (Arts. 51(c) and 253).

- ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997)**: **CEDAW** was used to frame guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace.
- ***Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin* (1980)**: the **ICCPR** was used to interpret the CPC on arrest for debt.
- *NALSA v. UOI* (2014): international principles were used to recognise the rights of transgender persons.
- *PUCL v. UOI* (1997): telephone tapping.

**(e) Committee reports and Law Commission reports**
These help identify the **mischief and the purpose** of the law (*R.S. Nayak*, 1984; *Rosy v. State of Kerala*, 2000; *Santa Singh v. State of Punjab*, 1976).

**(f) Other external aids**
- **Dictionaries**: used for the ordinary meaning of words that are not defined, but the **context prevails** over the dictionary.
- **Textbooks**, **foreign decisions**, and **historical background**.
- **Contemporaneous exposition**: how the law was understood when it was made (*contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege*).
- **Usage**: long-established practice.

## MODULE 4: APPROACHES, RESTRAINTS AND PRESUMPTIONS IN INTERPRETATION

### 1. Theoretical and ideological approaches
Judges approach statutes with different **theories** of interpretation:
1. **Textual / literal**: the text is supreme.
2. **Intentionalist**: find what the legislature actually intended when it enacted the law.
3. **Purposive / teleological**: read the law to achieve its purpose.
4. **Dynamic / evolutive**: the law adapts to changing times.
5. **Sociological** (Roscoe Pound): law as a tool of **social engineering**, so interpretation should promote social welfare.
6. **Realist**: law is what judges actually decide.
7. **Natural law**: interpretation should be consistent with justice and morality.

The judge's **ideology** (liberal, conservative, rights-oriented, welfare-oriented) also influences which approach they choose.

### 2. Judicial activism and judicial restraint

**Judicial activism**
Courts take an **active role**: they go beyond the literal text to protect **rights**, fill **gaps** left by the legislature, and hold the executive accountable.

Indian examples:
- ***Golak Nath* (1967)**;
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**: the basic structure doctrine;
- ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**: expansion of Art. 21;
- **Public interest litigation**: *S.P. Gupta* (1981), *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979), the *M.C. Mehta* environmental cases;
- ***Vishaka* (1997)**: guidelines in the absence of legislation;
- *Olga Tellis* (1985): the right to livelihood;
- ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)**: the right to privacy;
- ***Navtej Singh Johar* (2018)**.

**Merits:** protects rights, gives voice to the weak, ensures accountability.
**Criticism:** called **judicial overreach** or "judicial legislation". It is said to violate the separation of powers, and judges are **not elected**.

**Judicial restraint**
Judges **defer to the legislature and executive**. They decide only what is necessary, **presume laws are constitutional**, and do not replace policy choices with their own views.

Examples:
- *A.K. Gopalan* (1950);
- ***Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (2008)**: Justice Katju warned judges to know their limits and not try to run the government;
- *Almitra Patel v. UOI* (2000).

**Juristic activism and juristic restraint**
These terms describe the approach of **legal scholars (jurists)** rather than judges.
- **Juristic activism**: scholars who favour **creative, reform-oriented** interpretation. Examples: Upendra Baxi on PIL; Ronald Dworkin's idea of the judge as "Hercules" who seeks the best interpretation of the law as a whole.
- **Juristic restraint**: scholars who favour **positivist** interpretation, where judges declare the law but do not make it. Examples: H.L.A. Hart and John Austin.

**Conclusion:** a **balance** is needed. Activism is justified when fundamental rights are at stake or the other branches have failed. Restraint is the normal rule, out of respect for the **separation of powers**.

### 3. Presumptions in interpretation
Courts assume certain things about legislative intention **unless the statute clearly says otherwise**.

**(a) Presumption against ousting established jurisdiction**
The jurisdiction of **civil courts** is not lightly taken away. Any exclusion must be **express or clearly implied** (CPC s.9).

***Dhulabhai v. State of M.P.* (1969)** laid down the principles:
- When a statute makes a tribunal's order **final**, civil court jurisdiction is excluded **only if** an **adequate alternative remedy** is provided.
- Even then, a civil court can step in where the tribunal **did not follow the Act** or the basic principles of judicial procedure.
- A challenge that the Act is **ultra vires** can still be brought in a civil court.
- Exclusion of jurisdiction is **not readily inferred**.

See also *Secretary of State v. Mask & Co.* (1940).

Clauses saying an order "**shall be final**" or "**shall not be questioned in any court**" **cannot exclude** the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court under **Arts. 226 and 32**, because **judicial review is part of the basic structure** (***L. Chandra Kumar v. UOI*, 1997**).

**(b) Presumption against creating new jurisdiction or enlarging existing jurisdiction**
Courts presume the legislature does **not** intend to create **new jurisdiction** or **enlarge** the jurisdiction of an existing court, unless it uses **clear words**. Provisions conferring jurisdiction are **strictly construed**.

- ***A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak* (1988)**: even the Supreme Court cannot confer jurisdiction on a court where the statute has not given it.
- *Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan* (1954): a decree passed by a court without jurisdiction is a **nullity**.

**(c) Presumption about territorial extent of jurisdiction**
A legislature is presumed to legislate **only for its own territory**.
- **Parliament** may make laws with **extra-territorial operation** (Art. 245(2)), especially where there is a connection with India (*GVK Industries v. ITO*, 2011).
- **State laws** are valid outside the State's borders only where there is a **sufficient territorial nexus**. ***State of Bombay v. R.M.D.C.* (1957)**: a prize competition run from a Bangalore newspaper and circulated in Bombay had a sufficient nexus with Bombay. See also *Tata Iron & Steel Co. v. State of Bihar* (1958).

**(d) Presumption against violation of international law**
Statutes are presumed to be **consistent with international law** and India's **treaty obligations**. Where two readings are possible, the one consistent with international law is preferred. But if the statute is **clear** and conflicts with international law, the **statute prevails**.

***Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey* (1984)** adopted the **doctrine of incorporation**: rules of international law are part of national law **unless they conflict with an Act of Parliament**.

**Other presumptions**
- against **retrospective** operation;
- against **absurdity and injustice**;
- against taking away **vested rights**;
- that **mens rea** is required in criminal offences;
- that a statute **does not bind the State** unless it says so expressly or by necessary implication;
- in favour of **constitutionality**;
- that the legislature does not use **words without meaning**.

## MODULE 5: MODES OF INTERPRETATION OF THE CONSTITUTION

### 1. How constitutional interpretation differs from statutory interpretation
The Constitution is the **supreme law** (the *grundnorm*). It is a **living, organic document** meant to last for generations and to govern unforeseen situations. It must therefore be interpreted **broadly, liberally and purposively**, not like an ordinary statute.

Chief Justice Marshall in ***McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)**: "we must never forget that it is **a constitution** we are expounding", one "intended to endure for ages to come."

| Constitution | Ordinary statute |
|---|---|
| Supreme law | Subordinate to the Constitution |
| Interpreted liberally and dynamically | Interpreted according to the rules for its type (often strictly) |
| Involves political, social and moral values | Mostly technical |
| Hard to amend; basic structure limits apply | Easy to amend |

Indian cases: *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973); *Maneka Gandhi* (1978); *M. Nagaraj v. UOI* (2006), which described the Constitution as a living document.

### 2. Approaches to constitutional interpretation

**(a) Textualist**
Focuses on the **words** of the text. ***A.K. Gopalan* (1950)** read "**procedure established by law**" in Art. 21 literally, to mean any procedure laid down by an enacted law. This was later rejected in *Maneka Gandhi*.

**(b) Originalist**
Seeks the meaning the framers intended **at the time of framing**. It is popular in the USA (Justice Scalia) but used **sparingly** in India.

**(c) Harmonious construction**
Reconciles different provisions. Examples: reconciling **Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles** (*Re Kerala Education Bill*, 1957; ***Minerva Mills*, 1980**).

**(d) Historical interpretation**
Looks at the **historical background**: the freedom struggle, the Government of India Act 1935, and the making of the Constitution.

**(e) Constituent Assembly Debates (CAD)**
- **Earlier view:** *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) held the debates should not be used to interpret the text, though they could be referred to for historical background.
- **Later view:** the debates are a **legitimate aid** to understanding the framers' intention. Used in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), *Indra Sawhney* (1992), the ***Second Judges Case* (1993)**, and *S.R. Bommai* (1994).
- They are an aid, **not binding**.

**(f) Structuralist interpretation**
Reasons from the **structure** of the Constitution: federalism, separation of powers, democracy, and the relationships between institutions. The **basic structure doctrine** is the best example.

**(g) Pith and substance**
When a law passed by one legislature **incidentally touches** a subject in another legislature's list (Seventh Schedule), the court looks at the **true nature and character** (pith and substance) of the law. If, in substance, the law falls within the enacting legislature's competence, it is **valid** even though it incidentally affects the other field.
- ***Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce, Khulna* (1947)**: a State moneylending law that incidentally affected promissory notes (a Union subject) was upheld.
- ***State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951)**: the Bombay Prohibition Act was upheld even though it affected imports.
- The related doctrine of **colourable legislation** says a legislature cannot do **indirectly** what it cannot do directly (*K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa*, 1953).

**(h) Occupied field and repugnancy (Art. 254)**
For subjects in the **Concurrent List**, if a State law is **repugnant** to a Union law, the **Union law prevails** to the extent of the repugnancy. A State law that has received the **President's assent** prevails within that State (Art. 254(2)).

Tests of repugnancy (***M. Karunanidhi v. UOI*, 1979**; *Deep Chand v. State of U.P.*, 1959):
1. there is a **direct conflict** between the two laws; or
2. Parliament intended its law to be a **complete code** (it "occupies the field"); or
3. both laws deal with the **same subject matter**.

**(i) Residuary power**
Under **Art. 248** and **Entry 97 of List I**, Parliament has power over any matter not listed in the State or Concurrent Lists.
- ***Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon* (1972)**: wealth tax including agricultural land was upheld under the residuary power.
- *Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab* (1994): TADA was upheld partly under the residuary power.

**(j) Amending power (Art. 368)**
How far can Parliament amend the Constitution?
- *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965): Parliament could amend **any part**, including Fundamental Rights.
- ***Golak Nath* (1967)**: Parliament **cannot amend Fundamental Rights**.
- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**: Parliament can amend any part but **cannot destroy the basic structure**.
- ***Minerva Mills* (1980)**: a **limited** amending power is itself part of the basic structure.
- *I.R. Coelho* (2007): laws placed in the Ninth Schedule are open to basic structure review.
- *Fourth Judges Case* (2015): the NJAC amendment was struck down.

**(k) Moral reasoning and national ethos**
Interpretation guided by **constitutional morality**: the core values of dignity, liberty, equality and fraternity.
- ***Navtej Singh Johar* (2018)**: **constitutional morality prevails over popular or social morality**.
- *Sabarimala case* (2018).
- *Joseph Shine v. UOI* (2018).
- *Manoj Narula v. UOI* (2014).

**(l) Directive Principles as a source of interpretation**
DPSPs are **not enforceable** in court (Art. 37), but they are "**fundamental in the governance**" of the country. Courts use them to **give content to Fundamental Rights** and to judge whether restrictions are **reasonable**.
- *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951): DPSPs were treated as subordinate to FRs.
- Later cases moved to harmonising them:
  - ***Minerva Mills* (1980)**: the balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure;
  - ***Unni Krishnan* (1993)**: the right to education was read into Art. 21;
  - *Olga Tellis* (1985): livelihood;
  - *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984);
  - *State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat* (2005): Art. 48 was used to uphold a ban on cow slaughter.

**(m) Stare decisis**
Courts follow **precedent** for certainty and consistency.
- **Art. 141**: the law declared by the Supreme Court **binds all courts** in India.
- The **ratio decidendi** binds; **obiter dicta** are only persuasive.
- A **larger bench binds smaller benches**. A bench of equal strength that disagrees must **refer** the question to a larger bench (*Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v. State of Maharashtra*, 2005).

### 3. Pragmatism, the techniques of innovation, and overruling

**Pragmatist interpretation**
The judge considers the **practical consequences** of a decision: what works best for society. It is associated with **Holmes and Posner**. Many Indian PIL and environmental decisions reflect pragmatism.

**Techniques of innovation (or subversion) of stare decisis**
These are the methods judges use to **avoid being bound** by an earlier precedent:
1. **Distinguishing**: the facts of the present case are materially different.
2. **Per incuriam**: the earlier decision ignored a statute or a binding precedent.
3. **Sub silentio**: the point in question was not argued or considered.
4. **Treating the earlier statement as obiter**, not ratio.
5. **Narrowing** the ratio to its facts.
6. **Changed circumstances**.
7. **Reference to a larger bench**.
8. **Overruling**.
9. **Prospective overruling**.

**The Supreme Court's power to overrule**
The Supreme Court is **not bound by its own earlier decisions** (***Bengal Immunity*, 1955**). It may overrule a decision that is clearly wrong, through a **larger bench**. Examples:
- *Golak Nath* was overruled by *Kesavananda*;
- *A.K. Gopalan* was effectively overruled by *Maneka Gandhi*;
- ***ADM Jabalpur* (1976)** was overruled by *Puttaswamy* (2017);
- *Suresh Kumar Koushal* (2013) was overruled by *Navtej Johar* (2018).

**Prospective overruling**
The new rule applies **only to the future**. Past transactions decided under the old rule are left undisturbed. The doctrine comes from American law.

***Golak Nath* (1967)** applied it for the first time in India. It held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights **in future**, but the earlier amendments (the 1st, 4th and 17th) **remained valid**. It set these limits:
- it applies only to **constitutional matters**;
- only the **Supreme Court** can use it;
- the extent of retrospective effect is in the court's discretion.

Later cases extended it, including to statutes: *India Cement v. State of T.N.* (1990); *Ashok Kumar Gupta v. State of U.P.* (1997); *Somaiya Organics v. State of U.P.* (2001).

**Retrospective overruling**
Under the traditional **declaratory theory** (Blackstone), judges do not make law; they only **declare** what it always was. So when a precedent is overruled, the new rule applies **retrospectively**, as if it had always been the law. Cases already finally decided (res judicata) are not reopened.

In India, overruling is **ordinarily retrospective**. It is prospective only if the court **expressly** says so (*M.A. Murthy v. State of Karnataka*, 2003).

### 4. Advisory jurisdiction and precedent
Under **Art. 143**, the **President** may refer a question of **law or fact** of public importance to the Supreme Court. The Court **may** give its opinion. It must be heard by at least **five judges** (Art. 145(3)). The Court can **decline** to answer (*Ismail Faruqui*, 1994, on the Ayodhya reference).

**Is an advisory opinion binding?**
- ***Re Special Courts Bill* (1978)**: the opinion does **not bind the President**, but it carries **great weight** and **binds lower courts** under Art. 141.
- ***Re Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal* (1991)**: the opinion deserves respect and is **binding like any law declared** by the Supreme Court.
- In practice, the Government always follows it. Examples: the ***Third Judges Case* (Special Reference No. 1 of 1998)**, *Keshav Singh* (1964), *Re Berubari* (1960), *Re Kerala Education Bill* (1957), and the 2G natural resources reference (2012).

### 5. Objections to judicial review as anti-majoritarian
**The objection.** Alexander Bickel called this the "**counter-majoritarian difficulty**". When **unelected judges** strike down laws passed by **elected representatives**, they **override the will of the majority**, which seems **undemocratic**. Critics also argue that judicial review:
- weakens the separation of powers;
- lets judges impose their **personal values**;
- leads to **judicial supremacy**.

The NJAC judgment (2015), which struck down an amendment passed almost unanimously, is often cited as an example.

**The answers**
1. **The Constitution itself is the will of the people**, a higher law. When courts enforce it against a temporary majority, they enforce the people's deeper will (Hamilton in *Federalist No. 78*; *Marbury v. Madison*, 1803).
2. Judicial review **protects minorities and fundamental rights** from the "**tyranny of the majority**".
3. **John Hart Ely** argued that courts **reinforce democracy** by keeping the political process open and fair.
4. **Dworkin** described courts as a "**forum of principle**", where rights are decided on principle rather than politics.
5. In India, judicial review is **expressly provided** for (Arts. 13, 32, 226) and is part of the **basic structure** (*Kesavananda*; *Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975; *Minerva Mills*, 1980; *L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).
6. Courts limit themselves through **self-restraint** and the **presumption of constitutionality**.

**Conclusion:** judicial review is a necessary part of **constitutional democracy**, as long as it is exercised with **restraint and principled reasoning**.

## MODULE 6: CONSTRUCTION OF PENAL, TAXING AND REMEDIAL STATUTES

### 1. Strict construction of taxing statutes
**Rule.** Tax can be imposed **only by clear words**. Courts do not use **intendment, equity or presumption** in favour of a tax. If the words are **ambiguous**, the benefit goes to the **taxpayer**. Under **Art. 265**, no tax may be levied or collected **except by authority of law**.

Rowlatt J. in ***Cape Brandy Syndicate v. IRC* (1921)**: "In a taxing Act one has to look merely at what is clearly said. There is no room for any intendment. There is no equity about a tax. There is no presumption as to a tax. Nothing is to be read in, nothing is to be implied."

**Indian cases**
- ***A.V. Fernandez v. State of Kerala* (1957)**: if a person falls within the **letter of the law**, they must pay, however great the hardship. If they do not, they cannot be taxed, however much the case seems to fall within the **spirit** of the law.
- ***Mathuram Agrawal v. State of M.P.* (1999)**: the **charging provision** must clearly state **who** is taxed, **what** is taxed, and at **what rate**.
- *Govind Saran Ganga Saran v. CST* (1985): the four components of a tax (the taxable event, the person taxed, the rate, and the measure of value) must all be clear.

**Exemption notifications**
***Commissioner of Customs v. Dilip Kumar & Co.* (2018, Constitution Bench)** held:
- exemption provisions are interpreted **strictly**;
- the **burden** of showing that the exemption applies is on the **assessee**;
- any **ambiguity in an exemption is resolved in favour of the revenue**, not the taxpayer.

This overruled *Sun Export* (1997). Once eligibility is established, however, the exemption is applied **liberally**.

**Machinery provisions** (those dealing with assessment and collection) are construed **liberally**, so that the tax can actually be collected.

**Tax avoidance.** *McDowell & Co. v. CTO* (1985) held that **colourable devices** are not legitimate tax planning. ***Azadi Bachao Andolan* (2003)** and ***Vodafone* (2012)** held that **genuine tax planning** is permissible.

### 2. Strict construction of penal statutes and its limits
**Rule.** Penal statutes are construed **strictly in favour of the accused**. If two reasonable interpretations are possible, the court adopts the one that is **more lenient** to the accused (the **rule of lenity**). No one is punished unless the case falls **clearly within the words** of the statute.

**Basis**
- the maxim ***nullum crimen sine lege, nulla poena sine lege*** (no crime and no punishment without law);
- Arts. 20(1) and 21;
- the citizen is entitled to **fair notice** of what is forbidden;
- the **legislature, not the court**, defines crimes.

**Cases**
- ***Tolaram Relumal v. State of Bombay* (1954)**: where two reasonable constructions are possible, the court must choose the one that **exempts the subject from penalty**.
- ***W.H. King v. Republic of India* (1952)**: a provision of the Bombay Rent Act penalising a "premium" was strictly construed.
- ***Sakshi v. UOI* (2004)**: the court refused to widen the definition of rape by interpretation. That was a matter for the legislature.

**Limits of the rule**
1. Strict construction does **not** allow the court to defeat the **obvious intention** of the legislature or to adopt a **strained or unnatural** meaning.
2. It applies only where **real ambiguity** remains after using all other aids.
3. In **socio-economic offences** (food adulteration, hoarding, economic crimes), courts prefer a **purposive** reading that suppresses the mischief.
   - ***Murlidhar Meghraj Loya v. State of Maharashtra* (1976)**;
   - ***M.V. Joshi v. M.U. Shimpi* (1961)**: "butter" included butter made from curd;
   - *Kanwar Singh* (1965);
   - *Alamgir* (1959).
4. **Vague penal laws** may be struck down as unconstitutional (*Kartar Singh*, 1994; ***Shreya Singhal v. UOI*, 2015**, striking down s.66A of the IT Act).

### 3. Mens rea in statutory offences and the BNS
**Meaning.** ***Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea***: an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.

**Presumption.** **Mens rea is presumed to be required** for every offence, **unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication**.
- ***Sherras v. De Rutzen* (1895)**.
- ***Sweet v. Parsley* (1970)**: a landlady who did not know her tenants were using cannabis on the premises was **not guilty**.
- *Brend v. Wood* (1946).

**Indian cases**
- ***Nathulal v. State of M.P.* (1966)**: a dealer stored foodgrains while his licence application was pending, honestly believing it was allowed. He was **acquitted**, because mens rea was required and was absent.
- ***State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George* (1965)**: a passenger carrying gold **in transit** through India was **convicted** under foreign exchange and customs law, although he did not know of the RBI notification. Mens rea was **excluded by necessary implication** given the object of the law.
- *Ranjit D. Udeshi* (1965): knowledge of obscenity was not required for selling an obscene book.
- *Sarjoo Prasad* (1961): a servant was liable for selling adulterated food without knowledge.

**Strict liability offences** (no mens rea needed) are usually **regulatory or public welfare** offences: food and drugs, pollution, traffic, weights and measures, and some economic offences. The penalties are usually small.

**Mens rea under the BNS**
The BNS does not use the term "mens rea", but it uses mental-element words: **"intentionally", "knowingly", "voluntarily", "dishonestly", "fraudulently", "rashly", "negligently", "reason to believe"**. Examples:
- murder requires **intention or knowledge**;
- theft requires **dishonest intention**;
- cheating requires acting **fraudulently or dishonestly**.

The **General Exceptions** (ss.14–44) recognise the **absence of a guilty mind**: mistake of fact, accident, a child under 7 (s.20), an immature child between 7 and 12 (s.21), unsoundness of mind (s.22), and involuntary intoxication.

Some offences are effectively **strict liability**. For example, in sexual offences against a girl under 18, consent is immaterial. In kidnapping, the accused's belief about the victim's age is no defence (*R v. Prince*, 1875).

### 4. Vicarious liability in statutory offences
**General rule.** In criminal law there is **no vicarious liability**. A person is punished only for **their own act** done with **their own guilty mind**. The maxim *qui facit per alium facit per se* (whoever acts through another acts himself) belongs to **civil law**, not criminal law.

**Exception.** A **statute** may expressly or impliedly make one person liable for another's act.

**(a) Offences by companies.** Many statutes provide that when a company commits an offence, **every person in charge of and responsible for its business** at the time is also deemed guilty, unless they prove **lack of knowledge** or **due diligence**. Examples:
- NI Act s.141;
- Food Safety Act s.66;
- Environment (Protection) Act s.16;
- Drugs and Cosmetics Act s.34;
- IT Act s.85.

**(b) Employer and employee.** For example, a licence holder may be liable for the acts of their servants.

**Cases**
- ***Mousell Bros. v. London & North Western Railway* (1917)**: a company was liable for false invoices made by its manager. Whether vicarious liability applies depends on the **object and words of the statute**, the **nature of the duty**, and **who would ordinarily perform it**.
- ***Sarjoo Prasad v. State of U.P.* (1961)**: a servant who sold adulterated oil was liable even without knowledge.
- ***State of Maharashtra v. Syndicate Transport* (1964)**: a company can be criminally liable.
- ***Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement* (2005)**: a company can be prosecuted even for offences carrying **mandatory imprisonment**; a fine is imposed instead.
- ***Iridium India v. Motorola* (2011)**: a company can have mens rea through its **"directing mind and will"** (the doctrine of attribution).
- ***Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels* (2012)**: under NI Act s.141, a director can be prosecuted only if **the company is also made an accused**.
- ***Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI* (2015)**: a director is **not automatically liable** for the company's offence unless the statute says so or there is evidence of **active personal involvement**.
- *S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals v. Neeta Bhalla* (2005): the complaint must **specifically state** that the director was in charge of and responsible for the business.

### 5. Remedial vs penal statutes
**Remedial (beneficial or welfare) statutes** provide a **remedy or benefit**, or improve the condition of a class of people. Examples: Workmen's Compensation, Industrial Disputes, Maternity Benefit, Payment of Gratuity, Consumer Protection, Domestic Violence, and Rent Control Acts.

**Penal statutes** impose **punishment** (fines, imprisonment, forfeiture). Examples: the BNS and NDPS Act.

| Remedial statute | Penal statute |
|---|---|
| Gives a remedy or benefit | Imposes punishment |
| Construed **liberally**, in favour of the beneficiaries | Construed **strictly**, in favour of the accused |
| Doubt is resolved in favour of the beneficiaries | Doubt is resolved in favour of the accused |
| Can be retrospective | Cannot be retrospective to the accused's prejudice (Art. 20(1)) |

A single Act may contain **both** kinds of provision, and each part is read according to its nature.

### 6. Liberal construction of remedial statutes
**Rule.** Beneficial legislation is read **liberally** so as to **advance the remedy and suppress the mischief**. Where two meanings are possible, the court chooses the one that **helps the intended beneficiaries**.

**Limit.** Liberal construction cannot **rewrite the statute** or extend the benefit to people the statute clearly excludes.

**Cases**
- ***Alembic Chemical Works v. Workmen* (1961)**: in welfare legislation, prefer the interpretation that **benefits the workers**.
- *Workmen of American Express v. Management* (1985): Sundays were counted as working days for gratuity.
- *B. Shah v. Labour Court* (1977): Sundays were included in computing maternity benefit.
- ***Union of India v. Prabhakaran Vijaya Kumar* (2008)**: an accidental fall from a train was held to be an "**untoward incident**" under the Railways Act, so compensation was payable.
- *Bharat Singh v. Management of New Delhi Tuberculosis Centre* (1986).
- *Bangalore Water Supply v. A. Rajappa* (1978): a wide meaning was given to "industry".

## MODULE 7: COMMENCEMENT, OPERATION, EXPIRY AND REPEAL OF STATUTES

### 1. Commencement
A statute comes into force on:
1. the **date stated in the Act**; or
2. the **date notified by the Government** in the Official Gazette (different dates may be fixed for different provisions); or
3. if no date is given, the **date of assent** (GCA s.5).

It takes effect from the **midnight** at the beginning of that day.

**Courts cannot compel** the Government to bring an Act into force (***A.K. Roy v. UOI*, 1982**; *Aeltemesh Rein v. UOI*, 1988).

**Delegated legislation must be published** before it takes effect. Without publication it does not come into force (***Harla v. State of Rajasthan*, 1951**).

Example: the BNS, BNSS and BSA came into force on **1 July 2024** by notification.

### 2. Prospective, retrospective and retroactive operation
- **Prospective:** applies only to **future** acts, after the law begins. **This is the presumption** (*nova constitutio futuris formam imponere debet, non praeteritis*: a new law governs the future, not the past).
- **Retrospective:** applies to **past** acts and transactions. It may take away vested rights or impose new liabilities for things already done. It is **never presumed**. It must be **express or necessarily implied**.
- **Retroactive:** some writers distinguish this. The law operates only **for the future**, but it **relies on past facts**. Example: a law disqualifying persons convicted in the past from contesting **future** elections.

**Rules on retrospectivity**

1. **Substantive laws** (those affecting rights and liabilities) are **prospective** unless clearly stated otherwise. The **right of appeal is a vested right**, so a later law cannot take it away from a case already begun (***Garikapati Veeraya v. N. Subbiah Choudhry*, 1957**).

2. **Procedural laws** are generally **retrospective** and apply to pending cases, because **no one has a vested right in procedure**. The exception is where a procedural change affects vested rights or creates new disabilities (***Hitendra Vishnu Thakur v. State of Maharashtra*, 1994**).

3. **Penal laws** **cannot be retrospective** to the accused's **prejudice** (Art. 20(1)): no one can be convicted for an act that was not an offence when done, or given a heavier penalty than the law then allowed. But a **beneficial** penal law can apply retrospectively in the accused's favour.
   - ***Rattan Lal v. State of Punjab* (1965)**: the Probation of Offenders Act was applied to an offence committed before it came into force.
   - *T. Barai v. Henry Ah Hoe* (1983): a reduction in punishment benefits the accused.

4. **Declaratory and clarificatory laws** are retrospective, since they only declare what the law always was (*Zile Singh v. State of Haryana*, 2004).

5. **Validating laws** are retrospective. They can **remove the basis** of a court judgment, but the legislature **cannot simply overrule a judgment** (*S.R. Bhagwat*, 1995; *Madras Bar Association*, 2021).

6. **Taxing laws** can be retrospective if clearly stated, subject to Arts. 14 and 19. The 2012 Vodafone-related retrospective tax amendment was withdrawn in 2021.

7. ***CIT v. Vatika Township* (2014, Constitution Bench)**: the presumption against retrospectivity rests on **fairness**. People should be able to plan their affairs under the law as it stands.

### 3. Perpetual and temporary statutes
- A **perpetual statute** has **no fixed period**. It lasts until it is **repealed**. "Perpetual" does not mean it can never be repealed.
- A **temporary statute** lasts for a **fixed period** and **expires automatically** at the end of it, unless extended. Examples:
  - TADA (1985–1995);
  - annual Finance Acts;
  - **Ordinances**, which lapse **six weeks after the legislature reassembles** (Arts. 123 and 213) unless replaced by an Act.

A temporary Act may be **extended** by a new law passed **before** it expires. Once it has expired, it can only be brought back by a **fresh enactment**.

### 4. Effect of expiry of temporary statutes
1. **GCA s.6 does not apply to expiry.** Section 6 protects pending proceedings and liabilities only on **repeal**, and expiry is not repeal. So when a temporary Act expires, **pending prosecutions lapse**, and **no new prosecution** can be started for offences committed while it was in force, **unless the Act contains a saving clause**.
   - ***State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose* (1962)**: this is the general rule. But whether **rights and liabilities** survive also depends on the **construction of the particular statute**. Some may survive even without a saving clause.
   - ***Rayala Corporation v. Director of Enforcement* (1969)**: after a FERA rule lapsed, proceedings under it could not continue, since s.6 does not apply to expiry or omission.
2. **Things already done and completed** under the Act remain **valid**.
3. **Rules, orders and notifications** made under the Act **lapse** with it.
4. **Persons detained** under an expired detention law must be **released**.
5. If a temporary Act is **repealed before it expires**, **s.6 does apply**, because that is a repeal.
6. **Ordinances.** ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017, seven judges)**: rights and liabilities created by an Ordinance **do not automatically survive** its lapse. The test is **public interest and constitutional necessity**. **Repeated re-promulgation** of Ordinances is a **fraud on the Constitution** (also *D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar*, 1987).
7. **Omission vs repeal.** Is removing a provision ("omission") a repeal that attracts s.6?
   - *Rayala* (1969) and *Kolhapur Canesugar Works v. UOI* (2000) said **no**.
   - ***Fibre Boards (P) Ltd. v. CIT* (2015)** held that omission **is a kind of repeal**, so s.6 applies. *Shree Bhagwati Steel Rolling Mills* (2016) agreed.

### 5. Express and implied repeal
**Express repeal.** The new Act **expressly states** that the old law is repealed ("is hereby repealed", "shall cease to have effect"). Examples:
- **BNS s.358** repealed the IPC;
- **BNSS s.531** repealed the CrPC;
- **BSA s.170** repealed the Evidence Act.

Repealing and Amending Acts are used periodically to clear obsolete laws from the statute book.

**Implied repeal.** No words of repeal are used, but the new law is **so inconsistent** with the old one that **both cannot stand together**. The later law prevails to the extent of the conflict (*leges posteriores priores contrarias abrogant*: later laws abrogate earlier contrary laws).

Courts **presume against implied repeal**. The legislature is taken to know the existing law, and if it meant to repeal it, it would have said so. The court first tries to **harmonise** the two laws.

**Tests for implied repeal** (*Zaverbhai Amaidas v. State of Bombay*, 1954; ***Municipal Council, Palai v. T.J. Joseph*, 1963**):
1. Is there a **direct conflict**, so the two cannot stand together?
2. Did the legislature intend the new law to be a **complete code** on the subject?
3. Do both laws occupy the **same field**?

**Special and general laws.** ***Generalia specialibus non derogant***: a **later general law** does not impliedly repeal an **earlier special law** (*J.K. Cotton Spinning & Weaving Mills v. State of U.P.*, 1961; *Ethiopian Airlines v. Ganesh Narain Saboo*, 2011). A **later special law**, however, can override an earlier general one.

Repugnancy under **Art. 254** (Union and State laws in the Concurrent List) works like a form of implied repeal.

### 6. Consequences of repeal
**Common law position.** Under the old common law, a repealed statute was treated **as if it had never existed**, except for **completed transactions**. Pending proceedings failed. This was **harsh**, so **s.6 of the GCA** was enacted.

**GCA s.6.** Unless a **different intention** appears, a repeal does **not**:
- **(a)** **revive** anything not in force when the repeal takes effect;
- **(b)** affect the **previous operation** of the repealed law, or anything done under it;
- **(c)** affect any **right, privilege, obligation or liability** already **acquired, accrued or incurred**;
- **(d)** affect any **penalty, forfeiture or punishment** already incurred;
- **(e)** affect any **investigation, legal proceeding or remedy** relating to those rights and liabilities. These may be **started, continued and enforced** as if the repealing Act had not been passed.

**Principles**
1. **No revival.** If Act A repealed Act B, and Act A is later repealed, Act B does **not** come back unless the law says so (ss.6(a) and 7).
2. **Only accrued rights are saved.** A **mere hope or expectation**, or the bare right to take advantage of a repealed law, is not protected (*Abbott v. Minister for Lands*, 1895; *Lalji Raja & Sons*, 1971).
3. **Pending cases continue** under the old law, and **past offences can still be prosecuted** after repeal.
4. **Repeal and re-enactment.** ***State of Punjab v. Mohar Singh* (1955)**: when a law is repealed and re-enacted, s.6 applies **unless the new law shows a contrary intention**. The mere absence of a saving clause is **not** enough to exclude s.6. Prosecutions under the repealed Ordinance in that case could continue.
5. **References and continuity on re-enactment.**
   - **GCA s.8**: references to the repealed law are read as references to the re-enacted law.
   - **GCA s.24**: rules and orders made under the old law **continue** if consistent with the new one.
   - Example: BNSS s.531(2) **saves pending proceedings** under the CrPC.
6. **Repeal of an amending Act** does not undo the amendments it already made (s.6A; *Jethanand Betab v. State of Delhi*, 1960).
7. **Delegated legislation** made under a repealed Act **falls with it**, unless saved by s.24 or a saving clause.
8. **Unconstitutional laws.**
   - A law made **after** the Constitution that violates Fundamental Rights is **void from the start** ("still-born") (*Deep Chand v. State of U.P.*, 1959).
   - A **pre-Constitution** law that is inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is only **eclipsed**. It **revives** if the inconsistency is later removed (the **doctrine of eclipse**, *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.*, 1955).
