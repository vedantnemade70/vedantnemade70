# LAW OF EVIDENCE (Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023): Module-wise Explanation

> The BSA came into force on **1 July 2024** and replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (IEA). It has **170 sections**. Old IEA numbers are given in brackets.

---

## MODULE 1: INTRODUCTION TO LAW OF EVIDENCE

### 1. What the law of evidence does
Every case, civil or criminal, turns on **facts**. A judge was not present when the events happened, so they decide on the **material placed before them**. The law of evidence answers three questions:
1. **What facts may be proved?** This is **relevancy** (ss. 3–50).
2. **How must they be proved?** This is **proof**: oral, documentary and electronic evidence (ss. 51–103).
3. **Who must prove them, and how are witnesses examined?** This is **burden of proof and production of evidence** (ss. 104–169).

It is an **adjective (procedural) law** and a **lex fori**, meaning the law of the court where the case is heard.

### 2. Historical background
- **Ancient India**: Hindu law recognised three means of proof: **documents (lekhya), witnesses (sakshi) and possession (bhukti)**, along with **ordeals** (divine tests).
- **Muslim period**: oral testimony and the oath were central.
- **British period**: the presidency towns followed English law, while the mofussil areas had confusing, non-uniform rules. Several attempts were made to codify.
- **1872**: **Sir James Fitzjames Stephen** drafted the **Indian Evidence Act, 1872**, a clear and logical code that worked well for over 150 years.
- **2023**: the **BSA** replaced it. The structure and most of the language were kept. The main changes were updating the law for the **digital age** and removing colonial expressions.

**Objectives:** to prevent courts being misled by irrelevant matter, to bring certainty and uniformity, and to ensure fair and speedy trials.

**Scope (s.1):**
- Applies to **all judicial proceedings in or before any court**, including **courts-martial**, both civil and criminal.
- **Does not apply** to **affidavits** or to **arbitration** proceedings.

### 3. Structure and key provisions of the BSA

| Part | Sections | Content |
|---|---|---|
| I: Relevancy | 1–50 | Preliminary; res gestae; admissions; confessions; statements of persons who cannot be called as witnesses; judgments; expert opinion; character |
| II: Proof | 51–103 | Facts that need no proof; oral evidence; documentary and electronic evidence; exclusion of oral by documentary evidence |
| III: Production and effect | 104–169 | Burden of proof; estoppel; witnesses; examination of witnesses; improper admission |
| IV | 170 | Repeal and savings |

**Main changes from the IEA:**
- A "document" now **includes electronic and digital records** (emails, server logs, smartphones, SMS, websites, cloud data, voicemails, location data).
- "Evidence" includes statements given **electronically**.
- Electronic records can be **primary evidence**.
- **Secondary evidence** is widened to include oral and written admissions, and evidence of a person skilled in examining documents.
- A new **certificate format** for electronic evidence (s.63, in two parts).
- Sections are merged: IEA ss.24, 28, 29 became s.22; ss.25, 26, 27 became s.23.
- A **joint trial** now includes a trial **in absentia**.
- Colonial expressions are removed.

### 4. Basic definitions (s.2)
**Fact (s.2(1)(f)):**
- (i) anything, state of things or relation of things that can be **perceived by the senses** (a physical fact); and
- (ii) any **mental condition** a person is conscious of, such as intention, knowledge, good faith or opinion (a psychological fact).

**Fact in issue (s.2(1)(g)):** a fact from which the existence or non-existence of a **right, liability or disability** asserted or denied in the case **necessarily follows**.
- In a **criminal** case, the facts in issue are the **ingredients of the offence**. In a murder case, for example: did A cause B's death, did A intend it, and was there grave and sudden provocation?
- In a **civil** case, they are the **issues framed** from the pleadings.

**Relevant fact (s.2(1)(k)):** a fact connected with another in any of the ways described in ss.3–50. "Relevant" means **legally relevant**, not just logically connected.

The fact in issue is the **main fact to be proved** (*factum probandum*). Relevant facts are the **evidentiary facts** that help prove it (*factum probans*).

### 5. Kinds of evidence
**Evidence (s.2(1)(e))** covers:
- (i) **oral evidence**: statements by witnesses, including those given electronically; and
- (ii) **documentary evidence**: documents, including electronic records.

| Kind | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Oral** | What a witness says in court. It must be **direct** (s.55). |
| **Documentary** | Documents produced for the court's inspection, including electronic records. |
| **Real / material** | Physical objects, such as a knife, clothes or fingerprints. The court can ask for them to be produced (s.55 proviso). |
| **Primary** | The original document itself (s.57). |
| **Secondary** | Copies and accounts of the document, allowed only in specified situations (s.58). |
| **Direct** | Evidence of a witness who personally **saw, heard or perceived** the fact. |
| **Circumstantial** | Evidence of other facts from which the fact in issue is **inferred**. |
| **Hearsay** | What a witness heard from someone else. It is generally **excluded**. |

**Circumstantial evidence** can be enough to convict, but the chain must be complete. ***Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra* (1984)** laid down **five golden principles**:
1. the circumstances must be **fully proved**;
2. they must be **consistent only with guilt**;
3. they must be **conclusive** in nature;
4. they must **exclude every other hypothesis** except guilt;
5. they must form a **complete chain** that leaves no reasonable doubt.

**Hearsay** is excluded because:
- it is not given on oath;
- it cannot be tested by cross-examination;
- the original speaker's demeanour cannot be seen;
- it can be distorted as it is repeated.

It is admitted only in exceptions such as res gestae, admissions, confessions and dying declarations.

### 6. Proved, disproved, not proved
- **Proved (s.2(1)(j))**: the court **believes** the fact exists, or thinks its existence so **probable** that a **prudent man** would act on the supposition that it exists.
- **Disproved (s.2(1)(c))**: the same test, applied to the fact's non-existence.
- **Not proved (s.2(1)(i))**: neither proved nor disproved.

The **"prudent man" test** applies to all cases, but the **degree of probability** required differs:
- in civil cases, a **preponderance of probabilities**;
- in criminal cases, **beyond reasonable doubt**.

### 7. Witness (s.124)
A witness is a person who gives evidence. **Everyone is competent** unless they cannot understand questions or give rational answers because of tender age, extreme old age, or disease of body or mind. (See Module 5.)

### 8. Presumptions: may presume, shall presume, conclusive proof
A **presumption** is an inference the court draws about a fact from other proved facts.

| Type | Meaning | Rebuttable? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **May presume (s.2(1)(h))** | The court **may** treat the fact as proved unless disproved, **or call for proof**. This is discretionary. | Yes | s.119: a person found with stolen goods soon after a theft may be presumed to be the thief |
| **Shall presume (s.2(1)(l))** | The court **must** treat the fact as proved **unless disproved**. | Yes | s.118: dowry death; s.120: absence of consent in rape |
| **Conclusive proof (s.2(1)(b))** | On proof of one fact, the other **must** be treated as proved, and **no evidence to the contrary is allowed**. | No | s.116: birth during a valid marriage proves legitimacy; s.35: judgments in rem |

**Appreciation of evidence:** courts **weigh** evidence; they do not count witnesses (s.139). Minor discrepancies do not destroy credibility; what matters is whether the core of the testimony is true (*State of U.P. v. M.K. Anthony*, 1985).

## MODULE 2: RELEVANCE AND ADMISSIBILITY

### 1. Relevance vs admissibility (s.3)
Section 3 says evidence may be given **only of facts in issue and relevant facts, "and of no others"**.

| Relevancy | Admissibility |
|---|---|
| Based on **logic** and connection | Based on **law and policy** |
| Asks: is the fact connected? | Asks: can it be received in court? |
| ss. 3–50 | Rules of proof and exclusion |

**A relevant fact may still be inadmissible.** For example, a confession to the police is relevant but barred by s.23, and privileged communications are relevant but protected. *Ram Bihari Yadav v. State of Bihar* (1998).

### 2. Res gestae (ss. 4, 5, 6)
**Res gestae** means "things done" as part of the **same transaction**.

**s.4 (IEA 6):** facts that are **so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the same transaction** are relevant, even if they happened at slightly different times or places. What the accused, the victim or **bystanders said or did** during or **immediately** before or after the incident is admissible, even though it is technically hearsay.

**Test:** the statement must be **contemporaneous and spontaneous**, leaving **no time to fabricate**.

**Cases:**
- *R v. Bedingfield* (1879): a woman with her throat cut said "See what Harry has done". It was **rejected** because the act was already over. The decision is widely criticised.
- *Ratten v. R* (1972, Privy Council): the deceased's panicked phone call saying "get me the police" was **admitted**. The test is **spontaneity**.
- *Gentela Vijayavardhan Rao v. State of A.P.* (1996): a statement recorded **hours later** was **not** res gestae.
- *Sukhar v. State of U.P.* (1999): a statement made immediately after a shooting was admitted.

**s.5 (IEA 7):** facts that are the **occasion, cause or effect** of a fact in issue, or that show the **state of things** or **opportunity** for it. Examples: footprints, wounds, broken locks.

**s.6 (IEA 8):** **motive, preparation and conduct**.
- **Motive** explains why a crime was committed.
- **Preparation**, such as buying poison, shows planning.
- **Conduct before or after** the act is relevant if it influenced or was influenced by the fact in issue, for example **absconding**, hiding evidence, or keeping silent when accused.
- **Absconding alone is weak evidence** (*Matru v. State of U.P.*, 1971).

### 3. Identification: test identification parade (s.7)
Section 7 makes relevant facts that **explain or introduce** a relevant fact, or **establish identity**, time, place or the relationship of parties.

A **Test Identification Parade (TIP)** is held during investigation, usually by a magistrate, so that witnesses can pick out a stranger accused from among similar-looking people.

**Value of a TIP:**
- The **substantive evidence** is the **identification in court**. The TIP only **corroborates** it.
- A TIP is **not compulsory**, but if one is not held, an in-court identification of a stranger carries less weight.
- It must be held **promptly**, and the accused must **not have been shown** to witnesses beforehand.
- The accused cannot refuse to take part (this does not violate Art. 20(3)).
- TIP statements made before police are hit by s.181 BNSS (*Ramkishan Mithanlal Sharma*, 1955).

Cases: *Kanta Prashad* (1958); *Malkhansingh v. State of M.P.* (2003).

### 4. Things said or done by conspirators (s.8, IEA 10)
If there is **reasonable ground to believe** that persons conspired, then anything **said, done or written by any one of them in reference to the common intention**, **after the intention was first formed**, is relevant **against all of them**. This is based on the idea that each conspirator is the **agent** of the others.

**Limits:**
- There must first be **prima facie evidence** of a conspiracy.
- The statement must be made **while the conspiracy was alive**. Statements made **after arrest** or after the conspiracy ended are not covered (*Mirza Akbar v. King Emperor*, 1940).

Other cases: *Badri Rai v. State of Bihar* (1958); *State v. Nalini* (1999); *Navjot Sandhu* (2005).

### 5. "Otherwise irrelevant" facts (s.9, IEA 11)
Facts that are not otherwise relevant become relevant if they are:
- **inconsistent** with a fact in issue; or
- make it **highly probable or improbable**.

The classic use is the **plea of alibi**: if A was in Kolkata when the murder happened in Delhi, that fact is inconsistent with A being the murderer. The **accused must prove the alibi with certainty** (*Binay Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar*, 1997).

**The problem:** read literally, s.9 could let in almost anything, including hearsay and remote facts. Courts therefore apply it **narrowly**:
- the inconsistency or probability must be **high**;
- s.9 **cannot override** exclusionary rules such as hearsay or the bar on confessions.

### 6. Damages and custom (ss. 10, 11)
- **s.10**: in suits for damages, facts that help the court **fix the amount** of damages are relevant.
- **s.11**: when a **right or custom** is in question, the following are relevant:
  - **transactions** by which it was created, recognised or denied;
  - **particular instances** where it was claimed, exercised or disputed.
- A custom must be **ancient, certain, reasonable and continuous**.

### 7. State of mind and body; accident; course of business (ss. 12–14)
- **s.12 (IEA 14)**: facts showing a **state of mind** (intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, ill-will) or a **state of body** are relevant when that state is in issue. The state of mind must relate to the **particular matter**. For example, if a person is found with many other stolen articles, this shows **knowledge** that goods were stolen. A previous conviction becomes relevant only where the previous offence itself is relevant.
- **s.13 (IEA 15)**: when the question is whether an act was **accidental or intentional**, a **series of similar occurrences** is relevant. For example, if several insured houses of the accused caught fire, this suggests the fires were not accidents. The same reasoning was used in *Makin v. A-G for New South Wales* (1894), where several babies' bodies were found in a backyard.
- **s.14 (IEA 16)**: the existence of a **course of business** is relevant to show that an act, such as posting a letter, was done.

### 8. Admissions (ss. 15–21, 25)
**Definition (s.15):** a statement, **oral, documentary or electronic**, that **suggests an inference** about a fact in issue or relevant fact, made by the persons described in the Act.

**Who can make admissions (ss. 16–18):**
- a **party** or its **authorised agent**;
- a party suing in a **representative capacity** (such as a trustee);
- persons with a **proprietary or pecuniary interest** in the subject matter;
- **predecessors-in-interest**;
- persons whose **liability must be proved** against a party, such as a debtor whose liability a guarantor has undertaken;
- persons **expressly referred to** by a party for information.

**Proof (s.19):** admissions may be proved **against** the maker but **not in their favour** (self-serving statements are excluded). The exceptions are statements that would be admissible under s.26 if the maker were dead, statements of **state of mind or body**, and statements relevant otherwise than as admissions.

**Other rules:**
- **s.20**: oral admissions about the **contents of documents** are not relevant unless secondary evidence is allowed, or the genuineness of the document is in question. The same applies to electronic records.
- **s.21**: admissions made **"without prejudice"** in civil settlement talks are **not relevant**. This encourages compromise.
- **s.25**: admissions are **not conclusive proof**, but they may operate as **estoppels**.

**Value:**
- An admission is **substantive evidence**, and can be used even if the maker was not confronted with it in the witness box (*Bharat Singh v. Bhagirathi*, 1966).
- **Judicial admissions** (in pleadings or in court) are **binding** (s.53).
- **Extra-judicial admissions** can be explained away.

### 9. Confessions (ss. 22–24)
"Confession" is **not defined**. It is a type of admission made by an **accused**.

**Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor (1939):** a confession must **admit the offence in terms, or at least substantially all the facts** constituting it. An admission of an incriminating fact, even a very serious one, is not a confession.

**Palvinder Kaur v. State of Punjab (1952):**
- an **exculpatory** statement is not a confession;
- a confession must be **read as a whole**, not cut into parts the prosecution likes and parts it does not.

**Kinds of confession:**
- **Judicial**: made before a magistrate under BNSS s.183. It can be the basis of a conviction if voluntary and true.
- **Extra-judicial**: made to private persons. It is **weak evidence**, and its value depends on the trustworthiness of the person who heard it (*Sahadevan v. State of T.N.*, 2012).

**s.22: confession caused by inducement, threat, coercion or promise.** A confession is **irrelevant** if it was caused by an inducement, threat, coercion or promise that:
- comes from a **person in authority**;
- relates to the **charge**; and
- gave the accused reason to think they would **gain an advantage or avoid an evil**.

If the effect of the inducement has been **fully removed**, the confession becomes relevant. A confession is **not** inadmissible merely because it was made under a promise of secrecy, through a trick, while drunk, or without a warning.

**s.23: confession to police.**
- **s.23(1)**: **no confession to a police officer** can be proved against the accused. The purpose is to prevent torture.
  - "Police officer" is understood in substance: an officer with police powers counts.
  - *Tofan Singh v. State of T.N.* (2020) held **NDPS officers are police officers**.
  - Customs officers are not (*Barkat Ram*, 1962).
- **s.23(2)**: a confession made **in police custody** to anyone cannot be proved, **unless made in the immediate presence of a magistrate**.
- **Proviso (IEA 27): discovery.** If information from an accused in custody leads to the **discovery of a fact**, then **only that part of the information which distinctly relates to the fact discovered** can be proved.
  - ***Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor* (1947)**: the "fact discovered" includes the **place of concealment and the accused's knowledge** of it.
  - So "I will show the knife **hidden under my bed**" is admissible, but "**with which I killed X**" is not.
  - *Deoman Upadhyaya* (1960).

**s.24: confession of a co-accused.** When persons are **tried jointly for the same offence**, a proved confession by one that affects himself and others **may be taken into consideration** against the others. A trial in absentia counts as a joint trial.
- It is **not substantive evidence**, only **lending assurance** to other evidence.
- *Bhuboni Sahu* (1949): it is the weakest kind of evidence.
- *Kashmira Singh* (1952): first marshal the other evidence, then use the confession.
- *Haricharan Kurmi* (1964).

**Retracted confession:** a confession later **withdrawn** by the accused, who claims it was forced.
- It is still **admissible**, and a conviction can rest on it if it was **voluntary and true**.
- As a **rule of prudence**, courts look for **corroboration** in material particulars (*Pyare Lal v. State of Rajasthan*, 1963; *Subramania Goundan*, 1958).
- The problem for courts is deciding whether the retraction is genuine or an afterthought.

**Admission vs confession:**

| Admission | Confession |
|---|---|
| Defined (s.15) | Not defined |
| Civil and criminal cases | Criminal cases only |
| Made by a party, agent, predecessor and others | Made by the accused only |
| Suggests an inference | Directly acknowledges guilt |
| Not conclusive; may operate as estoppel | If voluntary and true, can be the basis of a conviction |
| Every confession is an admission | Not every admission is a confession |

## MODULE 3: STATEMENTS BY PERSONS

### 1. Statements by persons who cannot be called as witnesses (s.26)
Normally a person must come to court and testify. Section 26 is an **exception to hearsay**. It makes statements relevant when the maker is:
- **dead**;
- **cannot be found**;
- **incapable** of giving evidence; or
- cannot be brought to court **without unreasonable delay or expense**.

It covers eight situations:
- (a) a **dying declaration**;
- (b) a statement in the **course of business**;
- (c) a statement **against the maker's interest**;
- (d) an opinion on a **public right or custom**;
- (e) a statement about a **relationship**;
- (f) a statement in a **family deed or will**;
- (g) a statement in a document about a transaction under s.11;
- (h) statements of **feelings** of several persons.

### 2. Dying declaration (s.26(a))
A statement by a person about the **cause of their death** or the **circumstances of the transaction** that resulted in their death, in a case where **the cause of their death is in question**.

**Indian law differs from English law:**
- the declarant need **not** have been **expecting death**;
- it applies in **civil as well as criminal** cases.

**Basis:**
- *nemo moriturus praesumitur mentire*: a dying person is presumed not to lie;
- **necessity**: the victim is often the only witness.

**Principles:**
- ***Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay* (1958)**: a dying declaration can be the **sole basis of conviction** without corroboration if it is true and voluntary. A record made by a magistrate in question-and-answer form is preferred.
- ***Pakala Narayana Swami* (1939)**: "circumstances of the transaction" means circumstances with a **proximate relation** to the death. The deceased's statement that he was going to meet the accused was admitted.
- ***Laxman v. State of Maharashtra* (2002)**: a doctor's **certificate of fitness is not essential**. What matters is that the declarant was mentally fit.
- ***Paniben* (1992)** and ***Atbir* (2010)**: the declaration must not be **tutored** or imagined. If there are multiple declarations, they should be consistent. A declaration with serious infirmities needs corroboration.
- ***Sharad Birdhichand Sarda* (1984)**: the deceased's letters about cruelty, written weeks before her death, were admitted as circumstances of the transaction.
- A declaration by **signs or gestures** is valid.
- If the person **survives**, the statement is **not a dying declaration**. It can only corroborate or contradict their testimony.

### 3. Statements made under special circumstances (ss. 28–32)
- **s.28: books of account**, including electronic ones, regularly kept in the course of business. They are relevant but **not sufficient alone** to charge anyone with liability. In *CBI v. V.C. Shukla* (1998, the Jain Hawala diaries), loose sheets were not accepted as books of account.
- **s.29: entries in public or official records** made by a public servant in the course of duty, such as birth registers and school records.
- **s.30: maps and charts** published for sale or made under Government authority.
- **s.31: statements of public facts** in Acts and notifications.
- **s.32: statements of law** in law books, including digital ones.

### 4. How much of a statement to prove (s.33)
When a statement is part of a **longer statement, conversation, document, series of letters or electronic record**, only **so much as is necessary** to understand its nature and effect is proved. This is the **rule of completeness**: a statement should not be taken out of context.

### 5. Judgments of courts (ss. 34–38)
- **s.34**: an earlier judgment that **bars a second suit or trial** (res judicata or double jeopardy) is relevant.
- **s.35: judgments in rem**. Final judgments in **probate, matrimonial, admiralty and insolvency** matters confer or take away a **legal character**, and are **conclusive proof against the whole world**.
- **s.36**: judgments on matters of a **public nature** are relevant but not conclusive.
- **s.37**: **other judgments are irrelevant**, unless the judgment itself is a fact in issue. A civil judgment is generally not relevant in a criminal case on the same facts (*K.G. Premshanker*, 2002).
- **s.38**: a party may prove that a judgment was obtained by **fraud or collusion**, or delivered by a court **without jurisdiction**.

A judgment **in rem** binds everyone. A judgment **in personam** binds only the parties.

### 6. Expert opinion (ss. 39–45)
Judges cannot know everything. On matters of **foreign law, science, art, handwriting or fingerprints**, the **opinion of experts**, meaning persons specially skilled by study or experience, is relevant (s.39). The **Examiner of Electronic Evidence** under the IT Act is now expressly treated as an expert.

**Related provisions:**
- **s.40**: facts that support or contradict the expert's opinion.
- **s.41**: opinion on handwriting by a person **familiar with it**, and opinion of a **Certifying Authority** on electronic signatures.
- **s.42**: opinions on general custom.
- **s.43**: opinions on usages and tenets.
- **s.44**: opinion on relationship, shown by conduct.
- **s.45**: the **grounds** of an opinion are also relevant.

**Problems with expert testimony:**
- An expert is a **witness of opinion, not of fact**.
- The evidence is **advisory**: the **court decides**.
- Experts may be **biased** because one party pays them.
- Some fields are **inexact sciences**, handwriting in particular.
- Experts may **contradict each other**.

The court should not accept a bare opinion. The **reasons must be given** (*State of H.P. v. Jai Lal*, 1999).

Handwriting evidence can be acted on without corroboration if reliable, but caution is advised, and the court may compare writings itself (*Murari Lal v. State of M.P.*, 1980).

**Ocular (eyewitness) evidence prevails over medical evidence** unless the medical evidence **completely rules it out** (*Solanki Chimanbhai Ukabhai*, 1983).

### 7. Evidence of character (ss. 46–50)
"Character" means both **reputation and disposition**. Only **general** reputation and disposition can be proved, not particular acts.

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Civil case, to prove conduct (s.46) | **Irrelevant** |
| Criminal case: **good character of the accused** (s.47) | **Relevant** |
| Sexual offences: the victim's character or past sexual experience (s.48) | **Not relevant** on consent |
| Criminal case: **bad character of the accused** (s.49) | **Irrelevant**, unless the accused first gives evidence of good character (then it is allowed in rebuttal), or bad character is itself a fact in issue. A previous conviction counts as bad character. |
| Civil case: character affecting damages (s.50) | **Relevant** (for example, in defamation) |

## MODULE 4: DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE

### 1. Document and its proof
**Document (s.2(1)(d)):** any matter **expressed or recorded on any substance** by letters, figures, marks or any other means, intended to be used to record that matter. It **expressly includes electronic and digital records**, such as emails, server logs, files on computers and phones, messages, websites, cloud data, location data and voicemails.

**Proof of contents (s.56):** the contents of a document are proved by **primary or secondary evidence**.

### 2. Primary and secondary evidence (ss. 57–60)
**Primary evidence (s.57)** is the **document itself** produced in court. It also includes:
- each part of a document executed in **several parts**;
- each **counterpart**, against the party who signed it;
- each copy made by **one uniform process** (such as printing), as evidence of the contents of the others.

**New electronic rules:**
- a record stored in **multiple files** simultaneously or in sequence: **each file is primary evidence**;
- a record produced from **proper custody** is primary evidence unless disputed;
- a **video** stored and transmitted at the same time is primary evidence;
- **automated storage**, including temporary files, is primary evidence.

**Secondary evidence (s.58)** includes:
- certified copies;
- copies made by **mechanical processes**, and copies compared with them;
- copies compared with the original;
- counterparts, against parties who did not sign them;
- **oral accounts** by someone who saw the document;
- **oral and written admissions** *(new)*;
- evidence of a **skilled person** who examined voluminous documents *(new)*.

**Best evidence rule (s.59):** documents must be proved by **primary evidence**, except in the cases in s.60.

**When secondary evidence is allowed (s.60):**
- (a) the original is with the **opposite party**, or with someone beyond the court's reach, and it is not produced after **notice** (s.64);
- (b) its existence or contents are **admitted in writing**;
- (c) the original is **lost or destroyed** without the party's fault;
- (d) the original is **not easily movable**, such as an inscription on a wall;
- (e) it is a **public document**;
- (f) a **certified copy** is permitted by law;
- (g) it consists of **numerous documents** and only the general result is needed.

A party must **lay the foundation** for secondary evidence before using it. A photocopy is not automatically admissible (*H. Siddiqui v. A. Ramalingam*, 2011; *J. Yashoda v. K. Shobha Rani*, 2007).

### 3. Electronic and digital records (ss. 61–63) and the IT Act
This is the most important modern topic.

**s.61:** an electronic record **cannot be refused** merely because it is electronic. It has the **same legal effect** as a paper document.

**s.62:** contents of electronic records are proved under **s.63**.

**s.63 (IEA 65B):** any computer output, whether printed, stored on CD or pen drive, or produced by any **computer or communication device**, is treated as a document and is **admissible without producing the original device**, if these conditions are met:
1. the device was **regularly used** by a person in lawful control;
2. information of that kind was **regularly fed** in the ordinary course;
3. the device was **working properly**, or any fault did not affect the record;
4. the output reproduces the information fed in.

A **certificate** in the **prescribed Schedule format** must be given. **Part A** is signed by the person in charge of the device. **Part B** is signed by an **expert**. It identifies the record, how it was produced, and the device's details (including hash values).

**The case law story:**
1. ***Navjot Sandhu* (2005)**: said a certificate was **not essential**. This was later overruled.
2. ***Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer* (2014)**: s.65B is a **complete code**, and the **certificate is mandatory** for copies of electronic records. If the **original device** is produced, no certificate is needed.
3. ***Shafhi Mohammad* (2018)**: relaxed the rule where a party does not possess the device. This was overruled.
4. ***Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Gorantyal* (2020)**: **reaffirmed Anvar**. The certificate is a **condition precedent**. It can be filed **at any stage** before the trial ends. The court can summon the person who must issue it.

Other cases: *Tomaso Bruno* (2015), on the value of CCTV footage, and *Ravinder Singh* (2022), which held call records need a certificate.

**IT Act, 2000 links:**
- s.4: legal recognition of electronic records.
- s.5: legal recognition of electronic signatures.
- s.10A: contracts formed electronically.
- **s.79A**: Examiner of Electronic Evidence.

The IT Act also inserted the provisions on presumptions about electronic agreements, secure records, electronic signature certificates, electronic messages, and **5-year-old electronic records** (now BSA ss.85–87, 90 and 93).

### 4. Attested documents and proof of signatures (ss. 64–73)
- **s.64**: **notice to produce** must be given before secondary evidence is used under s.60(a), except in specified cases.
- **s.65**: the **signature or handwriting** of the alleged signer must be proved.
- **s.66**: **electronic signatures** must be proved, unless they are secure electronic signatures.
- **s.67: documents required by law to be attested** (such as a **will**, a mortgage, a gift deed): **at least one attesting witness must be called** to prove execution, if one is alive and available. For a **registered** document **other than a will**, this is not needed unless execution is **specifically denied**.
- **s.68**: if **no attesting witness** can be found, prove the handwriting of one attesting witness and of the executant.
- **s.69**: an **admission** of execution by a party is enough proof against that party.
- **s.70**: if the attesting witness **denies or forgets** the execution, it may be proved by other evidence.
- **s.71**: a document that did not need attestation is proved like an unattested one.
- **s.72**: the court may **compare signatures and handwriting**, and may ask a person present in court to write for comparison. It should do this with caution.
- **s.73**: verification of **digital signatures**.

In the case of wills, the person propounding the will must also **remove any suspicious circumstances** (*H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma*, 1959).

### 5. Public and private documents (ss. 74–77)
**Public documents (s.74(1)):**
- records of the acts of the **sovereign authority**, **official bodies and tribunals**, and **public officers** (legislative, judicial and executive), in India or abroad;
- **public records of private documents**, such as registered deeds kept in a sub-registrar's office.

**All others are private documents (s.74(2)).**

- **s.75**: the officer having custody must give **certified copies** on payment of fees.
- **s.76**: public documents are proved by producing **certified copies**.
- **s.77**: proof of other official documents (Acts, proclamations and similar) by the Gazette or official copies.

**Difference:** a public document can be proved by a **certified copy** directly. A private document needs **primary evidence** or must meet the conditions in s.60.

### 6. Presumptions as to documents (ss. 78–93)
**Shall presume:**
- certified copies are genuine (s.78);
- records of evidence and confessions are genuine (s.79);
- Gazettes, newspapers and private Acts (s.80), and electronic Gazettes (s.81);
- electronic agreements, secure electronic records and signatures, and electronic signature certificates (ss.85–87).

**May presume:**
- maps made under Government authority (s.82);
- law collections and law reports (s.83);
- **powers of attorney** executed before a notary (s.84);
- foreign judicial records (s.88);
- books, maps and charts (s.89);
- **telegraphic and electronic messages** (s.90): that the message received matches the one sent, but **not who sent it**;
- documents not produced after notice were duly executed (s.91);
- **30-year-old documents** from **proper custody** are genuinely signed and executed (s.92);
- **5-year-old electronic records** from proper custody bear genuine electronic signatures (s.93).

### 7. Exclusion of oral by documentary evidence (ss. 94–95)
**s.94 (IEA 91):** when a **contract, grant or disposition of property** has been put in writing, or the law requires a matter to be in writing, its **terms can be proved only by the document** (or secondary evidence where allowed). Oral evidence of the terms is excluded. This is the **best evidence rule** for documents.

**s.95 (IEA 92):** once the document is proved, **no oral agreement** between the **parties** can be used to **contradict, vary, add to or subtract from** its terms.

**Six provisos to s.95** allow oral evidence of:
1. **fraud, intimidation, illegality, mistake, want of capacity** or failure of consideration, i.e. facts that would invalidate the document;
2. a **separate oral agreement** on a matter the document is silent about, if consistent with it;
3. an oral **condition precedent**;
4. a later oral agreement to **rescind or modify** it (not if registration is required);
5. **usage or custom**;
6. facts showing how the language **relates to existing facts**.

**Differences between s.94 and s.95:**

| s.94 | s.95 |
|---|---|
| Applies to **everyone** | Applies only to **parties** and their representatives |
| Bars oral evidence **to prove the terms** | Bars oral evidence **to vary the terms** |
| Covers unilateral documents too | Covers bilateral documents |

Strangers to the document can show a variation (s.102). Oral evidence is also allowed to show that a document was a **sham**, never meant to be acted on (*Gangabai v. Chhabubai*, 1982; *Roop Kumar v. Mohan Thedani*, 2003).

### 8. Ambiguous documents (ss. 96–103)
- **Patent ambiguity**: the ambiguity is visible **on the face** of the document.
- **Latent ambiguity**: the language looks clear but becomes ambiguous **when applied to facts**.

| Section | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **s.96** | Patent ambiguity: **no** evidence allowed to explain it | "I sell my horse for ₹1,000 **or** ₹1,500" |
| **s.97** | Plain language that fits the facts: **no** evidence allowed to show a different meaning | |
| **s.98** | Plain language that is **meaningless** when applied to facts: evidence **allowed** | "My house in Kolkata" when the person has none there but has one in Howrah |
| **s.99** | Language that fits **one of several** persons or things: evidence **allowed** to show which was meant | "My white horse" when the person owns two |
| **s.100** | Language that **partly** fits one set of facts and partly another: evidence **allowed** | |
| **s.101** | Evidence allowed to explain **illegible, foreign, technical or local** words and abbreviations | |
| **s.102** | **Strangers** may prove a variation of the document's terms | |
| **s.103** | Savings for the Succession Act on wills | |

## MODULE 5: WITNESSES

### 1. Competency and compellability (s.124)
**Competency** means whether a person **can** testify. **Every person is competent** unless the court finds they cannot understand questions or give rational answers because of:
- tender years;
- extreme old age;
- disease of body or mind; or
- a similar cause.

A person of unsound mind is competent if they can understand.

**Compellability** means whether a person **can be forced** to testify. Generally every competent witness is compellable, except:
- **foreign sovereigns and diplomats**;
- the **President and Governors** (Art. 361);
- the **accused**, who is a witness only on their own request (BNSS s.353) and cannot be compelled (Art. 20(3)).

Some witnesses are compellable to appear but **privileged** from answering certain questions, such as judges and spouses.

**Child witness:**
- There is **no minimum age**. The test is understanding.
- The judge holds a **preliminary questioning (voir dire)** to check competency.
- Under the Oaths Act, a child **under 12** gives evidence without an oath, and it is still valid.
- Because children can be **tutored**, their evidence is examined carefully.
- Corroboration is **prudent but not required by law** (*Dattu Ramrao Sakhare*, 1997; *State of M.P. v. Ramesh*, 2011).

### 2. Witness unable to communicate verbally (s.125)
A witness who cannot speak may testify **by writing or signs** made in open court. This counts as **oral evidence**. The court must take the help of an **interpreter or special educator**, and the testimony must be **video-recorded**.

### 3. Parties, spouses and judges (ss. 126–127)
- **s.126**: in civil cases, the **parties and their spouses** are competent witnesses. In criminal cases, the **spouse of the accused** is competent.
- **s.127**: **judges and magistrates** cannot be compelled, except by order of a superior court, to answer questions about their **own conduct in court** or what they learned there as judges. They can be questioned about other events that happened in their presence.

### 4. Privileged communications (ss. 128–131)
Some relevant facts are **protected from disclosure** for reasons of **public policy**.

**(a) Matrimonial communications (s.128):**
- A married person **cannot be compelled** to disclose any communication made to them **during marriage** by their spouse.
- They are also **not permitted** to disclose it **without the consent** of the spouse who made it.
- **Exceptions**: suits between the spouses, and prosecution of one spouse for an offence against the other.
- The privilege covers **communications only, not acts**. In *Ram Bharosey v. State of U.P.* (1954), the wife could testify about what she **saw** her husband do.
- It continues **after divorce or death**, for communications made during the marriage. In *M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Ponnan* (1970), letters from a husband to his wife could not be used by her father in a defamation case.

**(b) Affairs of State (s.129):**
- No one may give evidence from **unpublished official records relating to affairs of State** without the **head of the department's permission**.
- However, the **court decides** whether the document really concerns affairs of State, and it may **inspect** the document privately.
- *State of Punjab v. Sodhi Sukhdev Singh* (1961).
- ***State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975)**: the "Blue Book" on the Prime Minister's security.
- ***S.P. Gupta v. UOI* (1981)**: correspondence on judges' appointments was disclosed. The court balances the public interest in disclosure against the harm caused.

**(c) Official communications (s.130):** a public officer **cannot be compelled** to disclose communications made to them **in official confidence** if disclosure would harm the **public interest**.

**(d) Information about offences (s.131):** magistrates, police officers and revenue officers **cannot be compelled to reveal the source** of information about offences. This **protects informers**.

### 5. Professional privilege (ss. 132–134)
**s.132:** an **advocate** cannot disclose, **without the client's express consent**:
- communications made in the course of their professional employment;
- the contents of documents they saw;
- the advice they gave.

The duty **continues after the engagement ends**. The same duty applies to interpreters and clerks.

**Exceptions:**
- communications made **in furtherance of an illegal purpose**;
- any fact the advocate observed showing a **crime or fraud** committed since the engagement began.

**s.133:** the privilege is **not waived** just because the client gives evidence. If the client **calls the lawyer as a witness**, it is waived only for the matters on which the lawyer is questioned.

**s.134:** a client **cannot be compelled** to disclose confidential communications with their legal adviser, unless the client offers themselves as a witness. Then they may be asked about communications needed to explain their own evidence.

**Reason for the privilege:** clients must be able to speak **freely** to their lawyers for justice to work.

### 6. Other provisions as to witnesses (ss. 135–137)
- **s.135**: a witness who is **not a party** need not produce their **title deeds** or documents that could incriminate them, unless they agree in writing.
- **s.136**: no one can be compelled to produce a document that another person in their place could refuse to produce, unless that person consents.
- **s.137**: a witness **cannot refuse to answer** a relevant question on the ground that the answer may **incriminate** them. But the compelled answer **cannot be used** to arrest or prosecute them, **except for giving false evidence**. This is a **statutory immunity** that balances the court's need for the truth against the witness's protection.

### 7. Accomplice evidence (ss. 138, 119)
An **accomplice** is a **guilty partner** in the crime (a principal or an abettor). An **approver** is an accomplice who has received a **pardon** and testifies for the prosecution.

**The two provisions appear to conflict:**
- **s.138**: an accomplice is a **competent witness**, and a conviction based on **uncorroborated** accomplice evidence is **not illegal**.
- **s.119, illustration (b)**: the court **may presume** that an accomplice is **unworthy of credit unless corroborated in material particulars**.

**How they are reconciled:**
- s.138 is a **rule of law**: a conviction without corroboration is legally valid.
- s.119(b) is a **rule of prudence**: it is unsafe, so courts insist on corroboration.

The accomplice is unreliable because they are a criminal, may want to shift blame, and hope for a pardon. In practice, the rule of prudence has become almost a rule of law.

**Corroboration must:**
- be **independent**;
- **connect the accused** with the crime in a material particular (*R v. Baskerville*, 1916; *Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan*, 1952).

One accomplice **cannot corroborate** another (*Haroon Haji Abdulla*, 1968). A **rape victim is not an accomplice**, and her evidence needs no corroboration (*State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh*, 1996).

### 8. Number of witnesses (s.139)
**No particular number of witnesses is required** to prove any fact. **Evidence is weighed, not counted.**

In ***Vadivelu Thevar v. State of Madras* (1957)**, the court divided witnesses into three kinds:
1. **wholly reliable**: a conviction can rest on one such witness;
2. **wholly unreliable**: rejected;
3. **neither wholly reliable nor wholly unreliable**: corroboration is needed.

## MODULE 6: BURDEN OF PROOF

### 1. Meaning and kinds
**Burden of proof** (*onus probandi*) is the obligation to prove a fact. It has two senses:
1. **Legal burden** (s.104): the burden of **establishing the case**. It **never shifts**. It lies on the party who asserts.
2. **Evidential burden** (s.105): the burden of **producing evidence**. It **shifts** back and forth during the trial as evidence comes in.

**Standard of proof:**
- **Civil cases**: **preponderance of probabilities**, i.e. which version is more probable.
- **Criminal cases**: **beyond reasonable doubt**. *Woolmington v. DPP* (1935): proving guilt is the prosecution's duty; this is "the golden thread" of criminal law. Reasonable doubt must be **real**, not fanciful.
- When the **accused** has to prove something, such as an exception or an alibi, they need only a **preponderance of probabilities** (*Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker*, 1964).

### 2. General rules (ss. 104–107)
- **s.104**: **whoever asserts must prove**. The person who wants judgment based on certain facts must prove those facts exist.
- **s.105**: the burden lies on the person **who would fail if no evidence at all were given** by either side.
- **s.106**: the burden of proving a **particular fact** lies on the person who wants the court to believe it, unless the law says otherwise. For example, the accused must prove their **alibi**.
- **s.107**: the burden of proving a fact **necessary to make evidence admissible** is on the person offering that evidence. For example, to use a dying declaration, the death must be proved. To use secondary evidence, the loss of the original must be proved.

### 3. Special burdens and exceptions (ss. 108–116)
- **s.108 (IEA 105)**: when an accused claims a **general exception** of the BNS (insanity, private defence, accident, intoxication) or a **special exception** (such as grave and sudden provocation), **the burden is on the accused**, and the court **presumes the absence** of those circumstances.
  - *K.M. Nanavati* (1962): provocation.
  - *Dahyabhai* (1964): insanity.
- **s.109 (IEA 106)**: a fact **especially within the knowledge** of a person must be proved by that person. For example, a train passenger must prove they had a ticket.
  - It **does not relieve the prosecution** of its basic burden (*Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer*, 1956).
  - In ***Trimukh Maroti Kirkan* (2006)**, when a wife died in the matrimonial home at night, the husband had to explain how. His **failure to explain** was a circumstance against him.
- **s.110**: a person shown to be alive within **30 years** is presumed alive. Whoever says they are dead must prove it.
- **s.111**: a person **not heard of for 7 years** by those who would naturally hear from them is presumed **dead**. Whoever says they are alive must prove it. **The date of death is not presumed.**
- **s.112**: once a relationship of **partners, landlord and tenant, or principal and agent** is shown, whoever says it has ended must prove it.
- **s.113**: a person **in possession** is presumed to be the **owner**.
- **s.114**: in a **fiduciary relationship** (lawyer and client, guardian and ward, doctor and patient), the person in **active confidence** must prove that the transaction was in **good faith**.
- **s.115**: presumption of guilt for certain offences in **disturbed areas**.
- **s.116**: **birth during a valid marriage**, or within **280 days** of its dissolution (the mother remaining unmarried), is **conclusive proof of legitimacy**, unless **non-access** between the spouses is proved.
  - *Goutam Kundu* (1993): DNA tests should not be ordered routinely.
  - ***Nandlal Badwaik* (2014)**: a reliable **DNA test prevails** over the presumption.
  - *Aparna Ajinkya Firodia* (2023): DNA tests only in exceptional cases, with the child's interest paramount.

**Presumptions in offences against married women:**
- **s.117**: **abetment of suicide** by a married woman within **7 years of marriage**, if she was subjected to **cruelty**. The court **may presume** abetment.
- **s.118**: **dowry death**. If, **soon before her death**, the woman was subjected to cruelty or harassment **for dowry**, the court **shall presume** the person caused the dowry death. "Soon before" requires a **proximate and live link** (*Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana*, 2021).

### 4. Court may presume certain facts (ss. 119–120)
**s.119 (IEA 114):** the court **may presume** any fact it thinks likely, considering the **common course of natural events, human conduct and business**. The illustrations include:
- a person found with **stolen goods soon after a theft** is the thief or a receiver;
- an **accomplice is unworthy of credit** unless corroborated;
- **official acts were regularly performed**;
- a state of things shown to exist **continues**;
- evidence that could be produced but is **withheld would be unfavourable** to the party withholding it (adverse inference);
- refusing to answer a question one is not legally required to answer suggests the answer would be unfavourable.

These are **rebuttable presumptions of fact**.

**s.120 (IEA 114A):** in a **rape prosecution**, once sexual intercourse is proved and the woman says in court that she **did not consent**, the court **shall presume** she did not consent.

### 5. Judicial notice (ss. 51–52)
**s.51:** facts of which the court takes **judicial notice need not be proved**.

**s.52** lists facts the court **must** notice, including:
- all **laws in force** in India;
- **international treaties** and agreements;
- proceedings of the **Constituent Assembly** and legislatures;
- **seals** of courts;
- names and signatures of public officers notified in the Gazette;
- countries and **flags** recognised by India;
- **divisions of time**, geographical divisions, and notified holidays;
- the **territory of India**;
- hostilities with other nations;
- names of court officers and advocates;
- the **rule of the road**.

The court may consult **reference books** for matters of history, literature, science or art, and may refuse to take notice unless the person produces such a book.

The list is **not exhaustive**. Courts also take notice of facts of **common knowledge**.

### 6. Facts admitted need not be proved (s.53)
Facts that the parties **admit at the hearing**, agree in writing to admit, or are deemed to have admitted in their **pleadings** need **no proof**. These are **judicial admissions**, and they bind the party. The court may still **require proof** if it wishes.

In criminal cases, an admission by the accused does **not** relieve the prosecution of its burden.

## MODULE 7: ORAL EVIDENCE

### 1. General principles
- **s.54**: **all facts may be proved by oral evidence**, except the **contents of documents and electronic records**.
- **s.55**: **oral evidence must be direct**:
  - a fact that could be **seen**: the witness says **"I saw it"**;
  - a fact that could be **heard**: the witness says **"I heard it"**;
  - a fact perceived by **another sense**: the witness says they perceived it that way;
  - an **opinion**: the person who **holds that opinion** must say so.

Experts' opinions in published treatises can be proved by the treatise if the author is unavailable. The court may require **material objects** to be produced.

### 2. Exceptions to the hearsay rule
Hearsay is excluded by s.55, but it is admitted in these situations:
1. **Res gestae** (s.4).
2. **Admissions and confessions** (ss.15–24).
3. Statements of persons who **cannot be called** as witnesses, including **dying declarations** (s.26).
4. **Evidence given in an earlier proceeding** (s.27), if the witness is now unavailable, the proceeding was between the **same parties**, the other side had the **chance to cross-examine**, and the issues were **substantially the same**.
5. Entries in **books of account and public records** (ss.28–32).
6. **Statements of conspirators** (s.8).
7. **Expert opinions in treatises** (s.55).
8. Opinions on **public rights and customs** (s.42).

### 3. Examination of witnesses (ss. 140–168)
**Order of examination:**
1. **Examination-in-chief**: by the party who calls the witness.
2. **Cross-examination**: by the opposite party.
3. **Re-examination**: by the calling party, only to **explain matters raised in cross-examination**. If a new matter is introduced with the court's permission, the opposite party may cross-examine on it.

(ss.142–143)

**Key rules:**
- The **judge decides admissibility** (s.141).
- Cross-examination **need not be limited** to what the witness said in chief (s.143).
- A person summoned **only to produce a document** is not a witness unless called as one (s.144).
- A witness may be **contradicted with their earlier written statements**, such as police statements. Their attention must first be drawn to the relevant parts (s.148). Under BNSS s.181, police statements can be used **only** this way (*Tahsildar Singh v. State of U.P.*, 1959).

**The court protects witnesses:**
- no question **without reasonable grounds** (ss.152–153);
- the court may forbid **indecent or scandalous** questions (s.154);
- it shall forbid questions meant to **insult or annoy** (s.155).

**Answers on collateral matters are final (s.156).** If a witness is asked about something only to test their truthfulness, their answer cannot be contradicted by other evidence. The exceptions are a **previous conviction** and questions about the witness's **impartiality**.

**Corroboration:**
- questions that tend to **corroborate** a relevant fact are admissible (s.159);
- a witness's **earlier statement**, made at the time of the event or to an authority, can be used to **corroborate** them (s.160).

**Production of documents (s.165):** a person summoned to produce a document must bring it, even if they object. The court decides the objection and may inspect the document, except one relating to affairs of State.

**Judge's power to ask questions (s.168):** the judge may ask **any question**, in any form, at any time, of any witness or party, and order **production of any document**, in order to discover the truth. The parties cannot object, and they need the court's permission to cross-examine on the answers.

**Limits on s.168:**
- the **judgment must be based on relevant facts** only;
- the judge **cannot compel answers** that are privileged;
- the judge cannot ask questions that would be improper under ss.151–152.

### 4. Leading questions (s.146)
A **leading question** is one that **suggests the answer** the questioner wants. For example: "You saw A stab B, didn't you?"

| Stage | Allowed? |
|---|---|
| Examination-in-chief and re-examination | **Not allowed** if objected to, **except** on introductory or undisputed matters, matters already proved, or with the court's permission |
| Cross-examination | **Allowed** |

The court can also allow leading questions in chief for a **hostile witness**.

### 5. Lawful questions in cross-examination (s.149)
In cross-examination, a witness may be asked questions to:
- **test their truthfulness**;
- find out **who they are and their position in life**;
- **shake their credit** by attacking their character, even if the answer may incriminate them.

**Exception for sexual offences:** questions about the victim's **general immoral character or past sexual experience** are **not allowed** to prove consent.

### 6. Hostile witness (s.157)
The Act does not use the word "hostile". Section 157 lets the court, at its **discretion**, allow the party calling a witness to **ask questions as in cross-examination**. This is used when a witness turns against the party who called them, or departs from their earlier statement.

**The evidence of a hostile witness is not wiped out.** The court can accept the parts that are **reliable** and consistent with other evidence.
- *Sat Paul v. Delhi Administration* (1976).
- ***Bhagwan Singh v. State of Haryana* (1976)**.
- *Koli Lakhmanbhai* (1999).

**Why witnesses turn hostile:** threats, money, long delays, and lack of protection. The **Witness Protection Scheme** (*Mahender Chawla v. UOI*, 2018; now BNSS s.398) and **perjury prosecutions** are the remedies. The Best Bakery case (*Zahira Habibullah Sheikh*) shows how serious the problem can be.

### 7. Impeaching the credit of a witness (s.158)
The opposite party, or the calling party with the court's permission, may **impeach a witness's credit** by:
- (a) evidence from people who know the witness and believe them **unworthy of credit**;
- (b) proof that the witness was **bribed** or received some other **corrupt inducement**;
- (c) proof of **earlier inconsistent statements**.

The old clause allowing attacks on a rape victim's character was **deleted in 2003** and does not appear in the BSA.

**Contradiction vs impeachment:** **contradiction** (s.148) is one way of impeaching credit. **Impeachment** is wider: it attacks the witness's **general trustworthiness**.

### 8. Refreshing memory (ss. 162–164)
A witness may **refresh their memory** by looking at a writing:
- made by themselves **at the time of the event or soon after**, while it was **fresh in memory**; or
- made by someone else and **read by the witness at that time**, knowing it to be correct.

A **copy** may be used with the court's permission. An **expert** may refer to professional books.

The witness may also testify to facts in the document if they are sure it was **correctly recorded**, even without a specific memory of the facts (s.163).

The **opposite party may inspect** the writing and **cross-examine** on it (s.164). When police officers refresh their memory from the **case diary**, the accused may see those entries.

## MODULE 8: ESTOPPEL

### 1. What is estoppel?
**s.121 (IEA 115):** when a person, **by declaration, act or omission**, has **intentionally caused or permitted another to believe a thing to be true** and **to act on that belief**, they (and their representatives) **cannot later deny the truth** of that thing in a case between them and that other person.

**Illustration:** A falsely leads B to believe that land belongs to A, and B buys it. Later A actually acquires the land and tries to set aside the sale, saying he had no title at the time. A **is estopped** from saying so.

**Essentials:**
1. a **representation** of an **existing fact** (not law, not a future promise);
2. made **intentionally**, or the person allowed the other to believe it;
3. the other person **believed it**;
4. they **acted on it** and changed their position to their **detriment**;
5. the case is **between the same parties** or their representatives.

**Nature:**
- Estoppel is a **rule of evidence**. It is a **shield, not a sword**, so it cannot create a cause of action (except promissory estoppel in India).
- There is **no estoppel against a statute** and **none on a point of law**.
- Origin: *Pickard v. Sears* (1837). See also *Sarat Chunder Dey v. Gopal Chunder Laha* (1892).

**Kinds:**
- estoppel by **record** (res judicata);
- estoppel by **deed**;
- estoppel by **conduct** (*in pais*), which is s.121;
- **promissory** estoppel;
- **tenant and licensee** estoppel.

**Tenant estoppel (s.122):** a **tenant** cannot deny the landlord's title **as it was at the start of the tenancy**, while the tenancy continues. A **licensee** cannot deny the licensor's title to possession at the time of the licence.

**s.123:** the **acceptor of a bill of exchange** cannot deny the drawer's authority to draw. A **bailee** cannot deny the bailor's authority. A **licensee** cannot deny the licensor's authority.

### 2. Estoppel distinguished from res judicata, waiver and presumption
| Estoppel | Res judicata |
|---|---|
| Arises from the **act of the parties** | Arises from the **decision of a court** |
| Stops a party **saying one thing and later the opposite** | Stops a party **litigating the same matter twice** |
| A rule of **evidence** (BSA s.121) | A rule of **procedure** (CPC s.11) that bars the court's jurisdiction |
| Based on **equity** | Based on **public policy**: finality of litigation |

**Waiver** is the **intentional giving up of a known right**. It is voluntary and needs **no detriment** to the other side. **Estoppel** needs the other party to have **relied on the representation to their detriment**. Waiver gives up a right; estoppel prevents denying a representation.

**Estoppel vs presumption:** a presumption is an **inference** the court draws. Estoppel **prevents a party from proving** the opposite of what they represented. It works like a conclusive presumption, but **only between the parties**.

### 3. Promissory estoppel
**Meaning:** if a person makes a **clear promise** intending it to be acted upon, and the other person **acts on it** and changes position, the promisor **cannot go back** on the promise if that would be **unjust**, **even without consideration**.

**Origin:** ***Central London Property Trust v. High Trees House* (1947)**, by Denning J. A landlord promised to accept reduced rent during the war, and was held bound for that period.

**Development in India:**
- ***Union of India v. Indo-Afghan Agencies* (1968)**: the Government was **bound** by its promise under an export scheme. The Government is not above equity.
- ***Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P.* (1979)** (Bhagwati J.): the leading case. The State promised a **sales tax exemption** for 3 years, and the company set up a factory relying on it. The State was **held to its promise**. **Executive necessity is not a defence**. In India, promissory estoppel can found a **cause of action**.
- ***Jit Ram v. State of Haryana* (1980)**: tried to weaken Motilal. It was later disapproved.
- ***Union of India v. Godfrey Philips* (1985)**: confirmed Motilal. There is **no estoppel against the legislature** when it legislates, and none that would force the Government to act **against the law**.
- ***State of Punjab v. Nestle India* (2004)**: the Government can withdraw a promise only if **overriding public interest** requires it.

**Limits:**
1. No estoppel **against a statute** or the legislature.
2. None where the promise was **beyond the Government's powers** or illegal.
3. The Government may withdraw for **overriding public interest**, with reasonable notice.
4. The promise must be **clear**.
5. The promisee must have **changed position**.
6. None where there was **fraud**.

**Difference from s.121:** s.121 concerns a representation of an **existing fact**. Promissory estoppel concerns a **promise about the future**. It is an **equitable doctrine** developed by courts rather than strictly a rule within s.121.

### 4. Improper admission or rejection of evidence (s.169)
**s.169 (IEA 167):** wrongly admitting or rejecting evidence is **not by itself a ground for a new trial or for reversing a decision**, if the appellate court finds that:
- **without the wrongly admitted evidence**, there was still **enough evidence** to justify the decision; or
- **even if the rejected evidence had been admitted**, it **would not have changed** the decision.

This applies in **both civil and criminal** cases. For example, if a confession to the police was wrongly admitted, the appeal court **ignores it** and checks whether the remaining evidence is enough to convict.

**On objections:**
- An objection to the **mode of proof** must be raised **at the time** the evidence is tendered, or it is lost.
- An objection that evidence is **inherently inadmissible** can be raised **at any stage**.
- *R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder v. Arulmigu Viswesaraswami & V.P. Temple* (2003).

The idea matches BNSS s.511 and CPC s.99: **no reversal unless justice has actually failed**.
